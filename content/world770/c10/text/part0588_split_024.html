CHAPTER XXV  

第二十五章  

Maria Silva was poor, and all the ways of poverty were clear to her. Poverty, to Ruth, was a word signifying a not-nice condition of existence. That was her total knowledge on the subject. She knew Martin was poor, and his condition she associated in her mind with the boyhood of Abraham Lincoln, of Mr. Butler, and of other men who had become successes. Also, while aware that poverty was anything but delectable, she had a comfortable middle-class feeling that poverty was salutary, that it was a sharp spur that urged on to success all men who were not degraded and hopeless drudges. So that her knowledge that Martin was so poor that he had pawned his watch and overcoat did not disturb her. She even considered it the hopeful side of the situation, believing that sooner or later it would arouse him and compel him to abandon his writing.
玛丽亚·席尔瓦很穷，贫穷的种种她都知道得清清楚楚。而对于鲁思，贫穷是一个表示生活条件不舒适的词。那就是她对贫穷的全部了解。她知道马丁很穷。她在自己的脑海中把马丁的状况和亚伯拉罕·林肯、巴特勒先生以及其他一些已获得成功的人们的少年时代联系在一起。尽管她也知道贫穷绝不是什么美事，但作为养尊处优的中产阶层，她觉得贫穷是有益的，它是一种有力的鞭挞，激励所有受苦役却不甘堕落、尚未绝望的人去争取成功。因此，当她得知马丁穷到连他的手表和大衣都当了时，她并不烦忧。她甚至将其视为形势有希望的一面，认为这迟早会唤醒马丁，迫使他放弃写作。
Ruth never read hunger in Martin's face, which had grown lean and had enlarged the slight hollows in the cheeks. In fact, she marked the change in his face with satisfaction. It seemed to refine him, to remove from him much of the dross of flesh and the too animal-like vigor that lured her while she detested it. Sometimes, when with him, she noted an unusual brightness in his eyes, and she admired it, for it made him appear more the poet and the scholar—the things he would have liked to be and which she would have liked him to be. But Maria Silva read a different tale in the hollow cheeks and the burning eyes, and she noted the changes in them from day to day, by them following the ebb and flow of his fortunes. She saw him leave the house with his overcoat and return without it, though the day was chill and raw, and promptly she saw his cheeks fill out slightly and the 有尽管fire of hunger leave his eyes. In the same way she had seen his wheel and watch go, and after each event she had seen his vigor bloom again.
鲁思从来没有读懂马丁脸上的饥饿。他的脸变得越来越瘦，两颊上原本浅浅的凹陷已经变得很深了。事实上，注意到他脸上的这一变化时，她感到很满意。从他身上除去了许多无用的肌肉和太像野兽一般的活力，他的脸变得精致了。它们既诱惑着她，又令她感到厌恶。有时候，当跟他在一起的时候，她发现他的眼中闪着一种不寻常的光芒。她倾慕这种光芒，因为它使马丁看上去更像一位诗人、一位学者——他想要成为那样的人，她也希望他成为那样的人。可是玛丽亚·席尔瓦从他那凹陷的双颊和燃烧的目光中读出了一个不同的故事。她注意到，随着马丁钱财的增多或减少，它们每天都在发生变化。她看到他出门时穿着大衣，回来时却没有穿。尽管那天天气阴冷，可她看出他的脸颊微微鼓了一些，眼睛里那股饥饿的火焰也消失了。同样地，她也发现他的自行车和手表不见了，而每一件东西不见了之后，她看到他的活力又充沛了。
Likewise she watched his toils, and knew the measure of the midnight oil he burned. Work! She knew that he outdid her, though his work was of a different order. And she was surprised to behold that the less food he had, the harder he worked. On occasion, in a casual sort of way, when she thought hunger pinched hardest, she would send him in a loaf of new baking, awkwardly covering the act with banter to the effect that it was better than he could bake. And again, she would send one of her toddlers in to him with a great pitcher of hot soup, debating inwardly the while whether she was justified in taking it from the mouths of her own flesh and blood. Nor was Martin ungrateful, knowing as he did the lives of the poor, and that if ever in the world there was charity, this was it.
还有他的辛勤刻苦她也看在眼里，她知道深夜里他的灯要点到什么时候。他是在工作呢！她知道他工作得比自己还卖力，虽然他做的是不同的工作。她也吃惊地发现他吃得越少就工作得越卖力。当她觉得他饿得实在厉害的时候，有时会装作无意地给他送去一长条刚烤的面包，开玩笑说他做不出这么好的面包，以此来笨拙地掩饰她送面包的行为。还有些时候，她会派其中一个孩子给他送去一大罐热汤。她内心经过一番挣扎，这样把食物从自己亲生骨肉的口中夺走到底应不应该呢？马丁也不是不感恩，因为他是如此了解穷人的生活。如果这个世界上确实还有仁慈的话，这就是了。
On a day when she had filled her brood with what was left in the house, Maria invested her last fifteen cents in a gallon of cheap wine. Martin, coming into her kitchen to fetch water, was invited to sit down and drink. He drank her very-good health, and in return she drank his. Then she drank to prosperity in his undertakings, and he drank to the hope that James Grant would show up and pay her for his washing. James Grant was a journeymen carpenter who did not always pay his bills and who owed Maria three dollars.
有一天，当她用家里所剩下的食物喂饱了她那群孩子后，玛丽亚又用她最后的十五美分买了一加仑廉价葡萄酒。马丁正好到她厨房里去打水，便被邀请坐下来喝一杯。他敬了一杯，祝她身体健康。她回敬了一杯。然后她又喝了一杯祝他事业有成，而他则祝愿詹姆斯·格兰特会现身，付给她洗衣服的工钱。詹姆斯·格兰特是一个四处流浪的木匠，他时不时地会欠账，一共欠了玛丽亚三美元。

Utterly differentiated creatures that they were, they were lonely in their misery, and though the misery was tacitly ignored, it was the bond that drew them together. Maria was amazed to learn that he had been in the Azores, where she had lived until she was eleven. She was doubly amazed that he had been in the Hawaiian Islands, whither she had migrated from the Azores with her people. Kahului, where she had first met her husband,—he, Martin, had been there twice! Yes, she remembered the sugar steamers, and he had been on them—well, well, it was a small world. And Wailuku! That place, too! Did he know the head-luna of the plantation? Yes, and had had a couple of drinks with him.
玛丽亚和马丁都空着肚子在喝这酸酸的新酿的葡萄酒。这酒立刻就进入了他们的脑袋。他们是完全不一样的人，但他们都在各自的痛苦中感到孤单。虽然他们的痛苦都被默默地忽略了，但这条纽带却将他们牵到了一起。得知马丁去过亚速尔群岛，玛丽亚惊呆了，十一岁之前她一直住在那里。一听马丁去过夏威夷群岛，她就更加吃惊了，她和她家人从亚速尔群岛移居之后就是搬到了那里。但是让她感到最为惊讶的是，他告诉她他去过毛伊岛，她是在那个特殊的小岛上长大成人、结婚出嫁的。卡胡卢伊，那是她第一次遇见她丈夫的地方——马丁他竟去过那里两次！是的，她记得那些运糖的轮船，他当过那些船上的水手——呦，呦，这个世界真小！还有怀卢库！那个地方也是！他会不会认识那个种植园里的头头呢？是的，跟他喝过几杯酒。
And so they reminiscenced and drowned their hunger in the raw, sour wine. To Martin the future did not seem so dim. Success trembled just before him. He was on the verge of clasping it. Then he studied the deep-lined face of the toil-worn woman before him, remembered her soups and loaves of new baking, and felt spring up in him the warmest gratitude and philanthropy.
于是，他们开始缅怀过去，将他们的饥饿浸没在酸酸的新葡萄酒里。对马丁来说，未来似乎并不那么暗淡无望。成功就在他面前颤动。他就要将它抓住了。他审视着自己眼前这个饱受苦难的女人那皱纹深嵌的脸，想起她的热汤和新鲜出炉的长条面包，感到体内涌起一股最为温暖的感激和博爱之情。
"Maria," he exclaimed suddenly. "What would you like to have?"
“玛丽亚，”他忽然大声说道，“你会想要什么呢？”

And from such largess, dispensed from his future, Martin turned and took his one good suit of clothes to the pawnshop. His plight was desperate for him to do this, for it cut him off from Ruth. He had no second-best suit that was presentable, and though he could go to the butcher and the baker, and even on occasion to his sister's, it was beyond all daring to dream of entering the Morse home so disreputably apparelled.
从他的未来送出这样一份厚礼之后，马丁转身拿出自己仅有的一套体面衣裳到当铺去了。他这么做是困境中的绝望之举，因为这将使他无法再跟鲁思见面。他没有第二套漂亮衣服可以上得了台面。尽管他仍然可以到肉铺和面包店去，甚至可以偶尔去一趟他姐姐家，但要穿着这么寒酸的衣服到莫尔斯家里去，他是做梦也不敢的。
He toiled on, miserable and well-nigh hopeless. It began to appear to him that the second battle was lost and that he would have to go to work. In doing this he would satisfy everybody—the grocer, his sister, Ruth, and even Maria, to whom he owed a month's room rent. He was two months behind with his type-writer, and the agency was clamoring for payment or for the return of the machine. In desperation, all but ready to surrender, to make a truce with fate until he could get a fresh start, he took the civil service examinations for the Railway Mail. To his surprise, he passed first. The job was assured, though when the call would come to enter upon his duties nobody knew.
他继续卖力工作，觉得痛苦而近乎无望。他似乎开始意识到第二场仗他也输了，他将不得不去找份活干了。他这样做就能使每个人都满意——杂货店老板、他姐姐、鲁思，甚至玛丽亚，他已经欠了她一个月的房租钱。他那台打字机也已经欠了两个月的租金了，出租商一直吵着要求他交租或者把机器还回去。绝望之下，他几乎只能投降了。他打算跟命运休战直到获得新的机会，便参加了铁路邮政所的文职考试。让他大为吃惊的是，他考了第一名。工作有着落了，尽管谁也不知道什么时候会通知他上岗。
It was at this time, at the lowest ebb, that the smooth-running editorial machine broke down. A cog must have slipped or an oil-cup run dry, for the postman brought him one morning a short, thin envelope. Martin glanced at the upper left-hand corner and read the name and address of the Transcontinental Monthly. His heart gave a great leap, and he suddenly felt faint, the sinking feeling accompanied by a strange trembling of the knees. He staggered into hes room and sat down on the bed, the envelope still unopened, and in that nonent came understanding to him how people suddenly fall dead upon receipt of extraordinarily good news.
就在这个时候，在他处于最低谷的时候，那台向来运作顺畅的“编辑机器”出了问题。肯定是某个轮齿打滑了，或者某个油杯的油用干了。因为有天上午邮差给他送来了一封薄薄的短信件。马丁瞥了一眼信封的左上角，看到是《洲际月刊》的名称和地址。他的心猛地一跳，他忽然觉得要晕厥过去了，伴随着这种下沉的感觉，他的膝盖奇怪地颤抖起来。他跌跌撞撞地走进自己的房间，坐在床上，信封还没有拆开。那一刻他才明白，为什么人们会因为接到异乎寻常的好消息而突然死去。
Of course this was good news. There was no manuscript in that thin envelope, therefore it was an acceptance. He knew the story in the hands of the Transcontinental. It was "The Ring of Bells," one of his horror stories, and it was an even five thousand words. And, since first-class magazines always paid on acceptance, there was a check inside. Two cents a word—twenty dollars a thousand; the check must be a hundred dollars. One hundred dollars! As he tore the envelope open, every item of all his debts surged in his brain—$3.85 to the grocer; butcher $4.00 flat; baker, $2.00; fruit store, $5.00; total, $14.85. Then there was room rent, $2.50; another month in advance, $2.50; two months' type-writer, $8.00; a month in advance, $4.00; total, $31.85. And finally to be added, his pledges, plus interest, with the pawnbroker—watch, $5.50; overcoat, $5.50; wheel, $7.75; suit of clothes, $5.50 (60 % interest, but what did it matter?)—grand total, $56.10.
这当然是个好消息啦。薄薄的信封里没有稿子，因此这必然是一封采用通知了。他知道《洲际月刊》收到的是哪个故事。是那篇《钟声》，他的一篇惊悚故事，足足有五千字。既然一流杂志都是一经采用就支付稿费，那里头就是一张支票了。每个字两美分——一千字二十美元，那这就是张一百美元的支票了。一百美元呢！在他拆开信封的时候，他欠下的所有账都一笔笔地涌现在他的脑海里——杂货店老板3.85美元,肉铺老板4美元，面包店老板2美元，水果店老板5美元，总共是14.85美元。然后还有房租费2.50美元，并预付下个月的2.50美元；两个月的打印机租金8美元，也预付一个月4美元；一共是31.85美元。最好还要加上他那些典当物的赎金和利息，付给当铺老板——手表5.50美元，大衣5.50美元，自行车7.75美元，一套衣服5.50美元(百分之六十的利息，可那又算得了什么呢。）———大总数是56.10美元。
He saw, as if visible in the air before him, in illuminated figures, the whole sum, and the subtraction that followed and that gave a remainder of $43.90. When he had squared every debt, redeemed every pledge, he would still have jingling in his pockets a princely $43.90. And on top of that he would have a month's rent paid in advance on the type-writer and on the room.
他看到——仿佛就在他跟前的空气里能看到——那些发光的数字：一个总数经过一次次相减给出一个43.90美元的剩余数字。等他还清了每一笔债，赎回了每一件典当物，他口袋里还会有一笔43.90美元的大钱叮当作响。除此之外，他还预付了一个月的房租和打字机的租费。

Five dollars for "The Ring of Bells"—five dollars for five thousand words! Instead of two cents a word, ten words for a cent! And the editor had praised it, too. And he would receive the check when the story was published. Then it was all poppycock, two cents a word for minimum rate and payment upon acceptance. It was a lie, and it had led him astray. He would never have attempted to write had he known that. He would have gone to work—to work for Ruth. He went back to the day he first attempted to write, and was appalled at the enormous waste of time—and all for ten words for a cent. And the other high rewards of writers, that he had read about, must be lies, too. His second-hand ideas of authorship were wrong, for here was the proof of it.
《钟声》才值五美元——五千个字才值五美元。不是两美分一个字，而是十个字一美分！而且编辑对它大加赞美。故事发表的时候，他才会收到支票。最低稿费每个字两美分，采用即付——都是一派胡言。这是个谎言，它将他引入歧途。早知如此，他绝不会去尝试写作的。他会去工作——为了鲁思而工作。他回忆他最初尝试写作的那一天，惊奇地发现自己竟浪费了那么多时间——全是为了这十个字一美分的结果。他曾经读到过有其他稿酬丰厚的作家，那肯定也都是谎话。他那些关于写作的二手信息是错误的，这就是证据。
The Transcontinental sold for twenty-five cents, and its dignified and artistic cover proclaimed it as among the first-class magazines. It was a staid, respectable magazine, and it had been published continuously since long before he was born. Why, on the outside cover were printed every month the words of one of the world's great writers, words proclaiming the inspired mission of the Transcontinental by a star of literature whose first coruscations had appeared inside those self-same covers. And the high and lofty, heaven-inspired Transcontinental paid five dollars for five thousand words! The great writer had recently died in a foreign land—in dire poverty, Martin remembered, which was not to be wondered at, considering the magnificent pay authors receive.
《洲际月刊》单册售价是二十五美分，它那高雅华美的封面表明它属于一流的杂志。它是一家正儿八经的体面杂志，从创刊以来一直连续出版。你瞧，每期杂志的封面上都印着一位世界级伟大作家的评语，声明着《洲际月刊》的神圣使命。而这些话都出自文坛巨星之口，他们都是在这份每期封面都相同的杂志里崭露头角的。然而，《思À明它属际月刊》这份受天之启示的高雅杂志竟然给的稿费是五千字五美元。马丁记得，最近有位在国外去世的大作家，死的时候穷困潦倒。考虑到作家们收到的“巨额”稿费，这也就没什么好奇怪的了。
Well, he had taken the bait, the newspaper lies about writers and their pay, and he had wasted two years over it. But he would disgorge the bait now. Not another line would he ever write. He would do what Ruth wanted him to do, what everybody wanted him to do—get a job. The thought of going to work reminded him of Joe—Joe, tramping through the land of nothing-to-do. Martin heaved a great sigh of envy. The reaction of nineteen hours a day for many days was strong upon him. But then, Joe was not in love, had none of the responsibilities of love, and he could afford to loaf through the land of nothing-to-do. He, Martin, had something to work for, and go to work he would. He would start out early next morning to hunt a job. And he would let Ruth know, too, that he had mended his ways and was willing to go into her father's office.
好吧，是他上了当。在作家以及支付给他们的稿费上，报社撒了谎，而他竟为此浪费了两年时间。但他现在不会再上这个当了。一句话他也不会再写了。他会去做鲁思想要他做的那些事，做大家都想要他做的那些事——找一份工作。去工作的念头让他想起了乔——那个在无所事事、流荡的乔。马丁重重地叹了一口，充满了羡慕之情。那么多天都是每天工作十九小时，这在他身上发生了强烈的反应。可是，乔没有恋爱，没有任何爱的负担，而且他可以在那个无事可做的地方到处闲混。而马丁呢，他有奋斗的目标，而且他就要去工作了。他明天一大早就去找工作。他还会让鲁思知道，他已经改过自新了，他愿意到他父亲的办公室去工作。
Five dollars for five thousand words, ten words for a cent, the market price for art. The disappointment of it, the lie of it, the infamy of it, were uppermost in his thoughts; and under his closed eyelids, in fiery figures, burned the “$3.85” he owed the grocer. He shivered, and was aware of an aching in his bones. The small of his back ached especially. His head ached, the top of it ached, the back of it ached, the brains inside of it ached and seemed to be swelling, while the ache over his brows was intolerable. And beneath the brows, planted under his lids, was the merciless “$3.85.” He opened his eyes to escape it, but the white light of the room seemed to sear the balls and forced him to close his eyes, when the “$3.85” confronted him again.
五千字才五美元，十个字才一分钱，这就是艺术在市场上的价钱。其中的失望，其中的谎言，其中的耻辱，在他想来已是到了极至。在他闭着的眼皮下，燃烧着几个火焰般的数字——“3.85美元”，那是他欠杂货店老板的钱。他打了个寒颤，觉得自己的骨头里一阵疼痛。特别是他的腰，疼得厉害。他的头也很疼，头顶疼，后脑勺疼，里头的脑子疼，似乎在膨胀着。而他的眉毛处更是疼得难以忍受。眉毛下面，他的眼睑底下，挥之不去是那个无情的“3.85美元”。他睁开眼睛想避开它，可是房间里那个白色的光芒仿佛要灼伤他的眼球，使他不得不闭上眼睛，然后那个“3.85美元”就又一次出现在了他面前。
Five dollars for five thousand words, ten words for a cent—that particular thought took up its residence in his brain, and he could no more escape it than he could the “$3.85” under his eyelids. A change seemed to come over the latter, and he watched curiously, till “$2.00” burned in its stead. Ah, he thought, that was the baker. The next s er.ds. um that appeared was “$2.50.” It puzzled him, and he pondered it as if life and death hung on the solution. He owed somebody two dollars and a half, that was certain, but who was it? To find it was the task set him by an imperious and malignant universe, and he wandered through the endless corridors of his mind, opening all manner of lumber rooms and chambers stored with odds and ends of memories and knowledge as he vainly sought the answer. After several centuries it came to him, easily, without effort, that it was Maria. With a great relief he turned his soul to the screen of torment under his lids. He had solved the problem; now he could rest. But no, the “$2.50” faded away, and in its place burned “$8.00.” Who was that? He must go the dreary round of his mind again and find out.
五千字才五美元，十个字才一美分——这个特别的念想在他的脑子里“住”了下来。他无法逃避这个念想，就像他无法逃避他眼皮底下的那个“3.85美元”一样。后者似乎发生了什么变化。他好奇地望着，直到代替它燃烧的变成了一个“2.00美元”。啊，他想，那是欠面包店老板的。下一个出现的数目是“2.50美元”。这个数字把他困住了，他深深地思索着，仿佛生死就悬在对它的解答上。他欠了某个人两美元五十美分，那是肯定的，但到底是欠谁的呢？找出其答案是专横而恶毒的宇宙交给他的任务。他的思绪就如无尽的长廊，他在里头游走，打开各种各样装着零碎记忆和知识的房间及卧室，寻找答案，却是白费力气。恍如过了几个世纪，他这才轻轻松松、毫不费力地记起来，那是欠玛丽亚的。他如释重负，将自己的灵魂转移到他眼睑下令人备受折磨的屏幕前。他把问题解决了，现在他可以休息了。但并不是如此，那个“2.50美元”渐渐消失了，在它原来的地方燃烧着一个“8.00美元”。那是欠谁的啊？他必须再沿着自己的思绪郁闷地走上一圈，把答案找出了。
How long he was gone on this quest he did not know, but after what seemed an enormous lapse of time, he was called back to himself by a knock at the door, and by Maria's asking if he was sick. He replied in a muffled voice he did not recognize, saying that he was merely taking a nap. He was surprised when he noted the darkness of night in the room. He had received the letter at two in the afternoon, and he realized that he was sick.
他不知道自己在这场探寻中跋涉了多久，但似乎是过了好长好长的一段时间他才在一阵敲门声中回过神来。是玛丽亚，她问他是不是病了。他用一个自己都认不出来的模模糊糊的声音回答，说他只是打个盹而已。他惊讶地发现房间里已经是夜色漆黑了。他下午两点就收到信了。他意识到自己生病了。
Then the “$8.00” began to smoulder under his lids again, and he returned himself to servitude. But he grew cunning. There was no need for him to wander through his mind. He had been a fool. He pulled a lever and made his mind revolve about him, a monstrous wheel of fortune, a merry-go-round of memory, a revolving sphere of wisdom. Faster and faster it revolved, until its vortex sucked him in and he was flung whirling through black chaos.
之后那个“8.00美元”又开始在他的眼睑下慢慢燃烧起来。于是他让自己回到了奴役之中。不过，他变聪明了。他没必要在他的脑子了游走一整圈。他刚才当了回傻瓜。他拖出来一根杠杆，让他的脑子里的那些东西围着他转，一个巨大的命运轮盘，一个记忆的旋转木马，一个智慧的滚动球体。它转得越来越快，直到它的漩涡将他吸了进去。他被抛到里面，旋转着穿过一片片黑色的混沌。
Quite naturally he found himself at a mangle, feeding starched cuffs. But as he fed he noticed figures printed in the cuffs. It was a new way of marking linen, he thought, until, looking closer, he saw “$3.85” on one of the cuffs. Then it came to him that it was the grocer's bill, and that these were his bills flying around on the drum of the mangle. A crafty idea came to him. He would throw the bills on the floor and so escape paying them. No sooner thought than done, and he crumpled the cuffs spitefully as he flung them upon an unusually dirty floor. Ever the heap grew, and though each bill was duplicated a thousand times, he found only one for two dollars and a half, which was what he owed Maria. That meant that Maria would not press for payment, and he resolved generously that it would be the only one he would pay; so he began searching through the cast-out heap for hers. He sought it desperately, for ages, and was still searching when the manager of the hotel entered, the fat Dutchman. His face blazed with wrath, and he shouted in stentorian tones that echoed down the universe, "I shall deduct the cost of those cuffs from your wages!" The pile of cuffs grew into a mountain, and Martin knew that he was doomed to toil for a thousand years to pay for them. Well, there was nothing left to do but kill the manager and burn down the laundry. But the big Dutchman frustrated him, seizing him by the nape of the neck and dancing him up and down. He danced him over the ironing tables, the stove, and the mangles, and out into the wash-room and over the wringer and washer. Martin was danced until his teeth rattled and his head ached, and he marvelled that the Dutchman was so strong.
极其自然地，他发现自己站在一个碾压机跟前，压上了浆了袖口。可是他注意到那些袖口上印着数字。这是用来标记衣物的新方法吧，他这么想，直到靠近了些看发现一只袖口上标着“3.85美元”。这时他才明白那是欠杂货店老板的账单，而那些在碾压机滚筒上飞快滚过的是他的账单。他想出了一个狡猾的主意。他要把账单都丢到地板上，那么就不用还这些债了。想到就干，他恶狠狠地把那些袖口揉成一团一团的，扔到肮脏不堪的地板上。那堆东西变得越来越大。虽然每张账单都复制了一千份，但他只认出了一张两美元五十美分的账单，那是他欠玛丽亚的。那说明玛丽亚是不会逼着他还债的，但他大方地决定他唯一会还的就是欠玛丽亚的帐。于是他开始从那堆丢出去的袖口里搜寻她的账单。他不顾一切地搜寻着，找了好几年。直到酒店经理——一个胖胖的荷兰人——进来的时候，他还在搜寻。他的脸上满是怒火，他叫嚷着，高亮的嗓门震得天摇地动：“我要从你的工资里扣除这些袖口的本钱。”那堆袖口变成了一座山，马丁知道他注定要干上一千年的苦力才能还清这些袖口钱。好吧，除了杀死经理、烧掉洗衣房没有别的办法了。可是壮硕的经理打败了他，抓着他的颈项将他拎上又拎下。他把马丁揪到熨衣板上，揪到炉子上、碾压机上，揪到外面的洗衣房里，揪到绞衣机和洗衣机上。马丁被晃得直到牙齿打颤，脑袋发痛，他惊讶于这个荷兰人的强壮。
And then he found himself before the mangle, this time receiving the cuffs an editor of a magazine was feeding from the other side. Each cuff was a check, and Martin went over them anxiously, in a fever of expectation, but they were all blanks. He stood there and received the blanks for a million years or so, never letting one go by for fear it might be filled out. At last he found it. With trembling fingers he held it to the light. It was for five dollars. "Ha! Ha!" laughed the editor across the mangle.

"Well, then, I shall kill you," Martin said. He went out into the wash-room to get the axe, and found Joe starching manuscripts. He tried to make him desist, then swung the axe for him. But the weapon remained poised in mid-air, for Martin found himself back in the ironing room in the midst of a snow-storm. No, it was not snow that was falling, but checks of large denomination, the smallest not less than a thousand dollars. He began to collect them and sort them out, in packages of a hundred, tying each package securely with twine.
“哼，既然如此，我要杀了你。”马丁说。他跑到洗衣房里去拿斧子，发现乔正在浆稿子。他试图让乔停下来，就用斧头向他挥去。可这武器在半空中挺住了，因为马丁发现自己又回到了熨衣间，处在一片暴风雪之中。不，那落下来的不是雪，而是大额支票，金额最小的也有一美元。他开始收集整理那些支票，每一百张捆成一扎，每一扎都用双股线捆得牢牢的。
He looked up from his task and saw Joe standing before him juggling flat-irons, starched shirts, and manuscripts. Now and again he reached out and added a bundle of checks to the flying miscellany that soared through the roof and out of sight in a tremendous circle. Martin struck at him, but he seized the axe and added it to the flying circle. Then he plucked Martin and added him. Martin went up through the roof, clutching at manuscripts, so that by the time he came down he had a large armful. But no sooner down than up again, and a second and a third time and countless times he flew around the circle. From far off he could hear a childish treble singing: "Waltz me around again, Willie, around, around, around."
他从这一任务中抬起头来，看到乔站在他面前，正在拿熨斗、浆过衬衫及稿子玩杂耍。他时不时地伸出手，添一捆支票到那些被抛起的各式物品中。抛掷物急剧增多，变成一个穿过屋顶的巨型圆圈，超出了视线。马丁朝他砍去，可是斧子被他一把抓走，加入了那个飞动的圆圈中。然后他抓起马丁，把马丁也扔了上去。马丁穿过屋顶去抓那些稿子，于是到他下来的时候他已经抓了大大的一抱。可刚刚下来他又被抛上去了，第二次、第三次……他在这个圆圈里飞了无数回。他能够听从到远处传来的一个高音童声，它在唱着：“跟我一起跳华尔兹，再转一圈，威利，一圈一圈又一圈。”
He recovered the axe in the midst of the Milky Way of checks, starched shirts, and manuscripts, and prepared, when he came down, to kill Joe. But he did not come down. Instead, at two in the morning, Maria, having heard his groans through the thin partition, came into his room, to put hot flat-irons against his body and damp cloths upon his aching eyes.
他从那一条由支票、上浆衬衫和上浆稿子组成的银河里重新找出了那把斧子，准备下去的时候杀了乔。可是他没有再下来。反而是玛丽亚，她透过薄薄的隔板墙听到了他的呻吟，于是凌晨两点的时候到他房里来，在他的身体上敷上“热熨斗”，在他疼痛的眼睛上放上湿布。
