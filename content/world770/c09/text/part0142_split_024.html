CHAPTER XXV  

第二十五章  

In two months more Frances had fulfilled the time of mourning for her aunt. One January morning—the first of the New Year holidays—I went in a fiacre, accompanied only by M. Vandenhuten, to the Rue Notre Dame aux Neiges; and having alighted alone and walked upstairs, I found Frances, apparently waiting for me, dressed in a style scarcely appropriate to that cold, bright, frosty day. Never till now had I seen her attired in any other than black or sad-coloured stuff, and there she stood by the window, clad all in white, and white of a most diaphanous texture. Her array was very simple, to be sure, but it looked imposing and festal because it was so clear, full, and floating. A veil shadowed her head and hung below her knee; a little wreath of pink flowers fastened it to her thickly tressed Grecian plait, and thence it fell softly on each side of her face. Singular to state, she was or had been crying; when I asked her if she were ready, she said, 'Yes monsieur,' with something very like a checked sob; and when I took a shawl, which lay on the table, and folded it round her, not only did tear after tear course unbidden down her cheek but she shook to my ministration like a reed. I said I was sorry to see her in such low spirits, and requested to be allowed an insight into the origin thereof She only said, 'It was impossible to help it,' and then voluntarily, though hurriedly putting her hand into mine, accompanied me out of the room, and ran downstairs with a quick, uncertain step, like one who was eager to get some formidable piece of business over. I put her into the fiacre. M. Vandenhuten received her, and seated her beside himself. We drove all together to the Protestant chapel, went through a certain service in the Common Prayer Book, and she and I came out married. M. Vandenhuten had given the bride away.
又过了两个月，弗朗西斯为她姑妈的服丧期满了。一月的一个早晨——那是新年假期的第一天——我只在范登赫顿先生的陪同下乘坐着出租马车去了内日河畔的圣母大街；我独自下车，上了楼，看见弗朗西斯显然在等我，她那单薄的穿着与那寒冷又有霜冻的明朗天气很不相符。直到今天为止，除了黑色或者暗淡色外，我还从未见她穿过其他颜色的衣服。而现在，她一袭白衣，站在窗边，那白衣的质地最为柔软。她的衣饰确实非常朴素，但显得华贵而喜庆，因为它如此清新、宽松而飘逸。一块面纱盖住了她的头，一直垂到膝盖以下；一顶用粉色花朵编成的小花冠将面纱牢系在她那浓密的希腊式大辫子上，因此面纱柔顺地垂在脸两侧。有点奇怪的是，她在哭，或者之前在哭；我问她有没有准备好是，她说：“好了，先生”，好像在强忍着啜泣；当我拿起放在桌上的披肩裹在她身上时，不仅她的泪水一滴滴不由自主地顺着脸颊往下流，她自己也像一根芦苇似的靠着我颤抖着。我说，我看见她情绪这么低落很是伤心，问她能否告诉我伤心的原因，她只是说：“我就是控制不住，”然后主动，但又有些慌乱地将手放入我的手中，陪我走出房间，摇晃着匆匆跑下楼，就像是一个急于完成一件可怕的事情的人。我将她抱入出租马车。范登赫顿先生接住了她，让她坐在自己身旁。我们一起乘马车到了新教教堂，完成了英国国教祈祷书所记录的宗教仪式，走出教堂时，我们就是夫妻了。范登赫顿先生将新娘交给了我。
We took no bridal trip. Our modesty, screened by the peaceful obscurity of our station and the pleasant isolation of our circumstances, did not exact that additional precaution. We repaired at once to a small house I had taken in the faubourg nearest to that part of the city where the scene of our avocations lay.
我们没有新婚旅行。因为我们本就朴素，地位又低下，默默无闻的。这与世隔绝之境倒也惬意，便不再强求那额外的麻烦。我们马上就搬进了我在近郊租的一间小房子，那边离我们俩工作的学校都很近。
Three or four hours after the wedding ceremony, Frances, divested of her bridal snow, and attired in a pretty lilac gown of warmer materials, a piquant black silk apron, and a lace collar with some finishing decoration of lilac ribbon, was kneeling on the carpet of a neatly furnished though not spacious parlour, arranging on the shelves of a chiffonier some books which I handed to her from the table. It was snowing fast out of doors; the afternoon had turned out wild and cold; the leaden sky seemed full of drifts, and the street was already ankle-deep in the white downfall. Our fire burned bright, our new habitation looked brilliantly clean and fresh, the furniture was all arranged, and there were but some articles of glass, china, books, etc., to put in order. Frances found in this business occupation till tea-time, and then, after I had distinctly instructed her how to make a cup of tea in rational English style, and after she had got over the dismay occasioned by seeing such an extravagant amount of material put into the pot, she administered to me a proper British repast, at which there wanted neither candies nor urn, firelight nor comfort.
结婚仪式结束后三四个小时，弗朗西斯便脱下雪白的新娘礼服，穿上一条漂亮的、质地更暖和的淡紫色长袍，系上一条可爱的黑色丝绸围裙，又披上一条镶着淡紫色丝带的蕾丝披肩，在这间并不宽敞但布置得井然有序的客厅里，跪在地毯上将我从桌上拿起来递给她的书摆到五斗橱里。屋外雪下得很急；下午成了暴风雪，天气变得很冷；灰蒙蒙的天气好像充斥着雪片，街上的积雪已经深及脚踝了。我们的炉火烧得很旺，我们的新家看起来非常干净明亮，一派新气象，家具都已经摆好了，只剩下一些玻璃器皿，瓷器和书籍等需要整理。弗朗西斯一直到吃茶点时才忙完，之后我特意亲自教她怎么用地道的英国方式来煮茶，看见我往茶壶里放那么多茶叶，她吃了一惊，但冷静下来以后，她还给我张罗了一道地道的英式餐，既不缺糖果又不缺茶壶，既有火光又有舒适。
Our week's holiday glided by, and we readdressed ourselves to labour. Both my wife and I began in good earnest with the notion that we were working people, destined to earn our bread by exertion, and that of the most assiduous kind. Our days were thoroughly occupied. We used to part every morning at eight o'clock, and not meet again till five p.m.; but into what sweet rest did the turmoil of each busy day decline! Looking down the vista of memory, I see the evenings passed in that little parlour like a long string of rubies circling the dusk brow of the past. Unvaried were they as each cut gem, and like each gem brilliant and burning.
一个星期的假期转瞬即逝，我们又投入了工作中。我和妻子从一开始就都很用心地工作，我们知道自己是劳苦大众，注定要竭尽全力赚面包钱，而教师这个职业又是最辛苦的工作。我们总是一天到晚忙于工作。我们总是早上八点分别，直到下午五点才再见面。但是劳累了一天之后，我们的休息是多么甜蜜呀！追忆起那一幕幕的往事时，我感觉在那个小客厅里度过的一个个夜晚，就像一长串红宝石盘绕在往昔那昏暗的眉头上。那一长串红宝石颗颗都像打磨过的宝石一样，颗颗都灿烂夺目，如燃烧的火焰。
A year and a half passed. One morning (it was a fête, and we had the day to ourselves) Frances said to me, with a suddenness peculiar to her when she had been thinking long on a subject, and at last, having come to a conclusion, wished to test its soundness by the touchstone of my judgment, 'I don't work enough.' 
一年半悄然而过。某天早上（那天是节日，我们不用上课）弗朗西斯突然奇怪地对我说：“我的工作不够。”每当她长时间思考一个问题，最终得到结论时就是这样的。她想用我的判断作为试金石来看看自己的想法是否正确。
'What now?' demanded I, looking up from my coffee, which I had been deliberately stirring while enjoying, in anticipation, a walk I proposed to take with Frances that fine summer day (it was June) to a certain farmhouse in the country, where we were to dine; 'what now?' and I saw at once, in the serious ardour of her face, a project of vital importance.
“怎么了？”我从咖啡杯上抬头问道。我刚刚正一边从容不迫地搅拌着咖啡，一边愉快地想着提过的郊游计划，也就是要在那个美好的夏日（当时已是六月）带着弗朗西斯散步到乡下一间农舍去，并在那里用餐；“你说什么？”我立马从她那严肃的神情里看出她有一件至关重要的事要跟我谈。
'I am not satisfied,' returned she. 'You are now earning eight thousand francs a year' (it was true; my efforts, punctuality, the fame of my pupils' progress, the publicity of my station, had so far helped me on), 'while I am still at my miserable twelve hundred francs. I can do better, and I will.' 
“我不满意。”她回答说。“您现在每年赚八千法郎”（这是真的，我工作勤奋，又很守时，学生进步的声誉以及工作的公众影响，迄今为止都在帮助我前进），“而我每年还是那可怜的一千两百法郎。我可以做得更好，我想做得更好。”
'You work as long and as diligently as I do, Frances.' 
“你和我工作一样久，一样努力，弗朗西斯。”
'Yes, monsieur; but I am not working in the right way, and I am convinced of it.' 
“是的，先生；但是我工作的方式不对，这一点我很清楚。”
'You wish to change—you have a plan for progress in your mind. Go and put on your bonnet; and, while we take our walk, you shall tell me of it.' 
“你想改变——你脑海里现在已经有了改进的计划。你去戴上帽子，我们一边散步，你一边告诉我你的想法。”
'Yes, monsieur.' 
“是的，先生。”
She went—as docile as a well-trained child. She was a curious mixture of tractability and firmness. I sat thinking about her, and wondering what her plan could be, when she re-entered.
她就像一个有教养的孩子一样乖顺地去了。她就是温顺与刚强的奇怪的混合体。我坐在那儿想着她，考虑着她的计划会是什么样子，这时她又回来了。
'Monsieur, I have given Minnie (our bonne) leave to go out too, as it is so very fine; so will you be kind enough to lock the door, and take the key with you?' 
“先生，我给明妮（我们的保姆）放了假，让她也出去走走，因为天气这么好；麻烦您锁门，带上钥匙，好吗？”
'Kiss me, Mrs. Crimsworth,' was my not very apposite reply; but she looked so engaging in her light summer dress and little cottage bonnet, and her manner in speaking to me was then, as always, so unaffectedly and suavely respectful, that my heart expanded at the sight of her, and a kiss seemed necessary to content its importunity.
“亲我吧，克里姆斯沃思夫人，”我的回答并不恰当；但她穿着清爽的夏装，戴上小太阳帽，看上去十分迷人，而且她和我说话的态度还像往常一样真挚温和，尊重有礼，一看到她我就会心花怒放，必须要一个吻来满足我内心的渴望。
'There, monsieur.' 
“好了，先生。”
'Why do you always call me "monsieur" ? Say "William." ' 
“你怎么总是叫我‘先生’呢？叫‘威廉’。”
'I cannot pronounce your W. Besides, "monsieur" belongs to you; I like it best.' 
“我发不出你的W音，而且‘先生’这个称呼专属于你啊，我最喜欢它。”
Minnie having departed in clean cap and smart shawl, we too set out, leaving the house solitary and silent—silent, at least, but for the ticking of the clock. We were soon clear of Brussels; the fields received us, and then the lanes, remote from carriage-resounding chausées. Ere long we came upon a nook, so rural, green, and secluded it might have been a spot in some pastoral English province; a bank of short and mossy grass, under a hawthorn, offered a seat too tempting to be declined. We took it, and when we had admired and examined some English-looking wild flowers growing at our feet, I recalled Frances' attention and my own to the topic touched on at breakfast.
明妮戴上干净的帽子，披上漂亮的披肩离开后，我们也出发了，留下一栋孤独而安静的房子——如果不是钟表的滴答声，房子起码是安静的。我们很快便走出了布鲁塞尔；迎接我们的是田野以及乡间小径，远离了车声隆隆的大马路。不久我们就到了一片幽静处，很有乡土风情，树木郁郁葱葱，与世隔绝，看上去像是英国某个充满田园风光的省份的一处景点；山楂树下有一个长满小草和生苔的斜坡，形成了一个座位，十分诱人，让人不忍拒绝。我们坐了下来，赏玩了脚下的英式的野花后，我又提起了早餐时俩人所谈到的话题。
'What was her plan?' A natural one—the next step to be mounted by us, or at least by her, if she wanted to rise in her profession. She proposed to begin a school. We already had the means for commencing on a careful scale, having lived greatly within our income. We possessed, too, by this time, an extensive and eligible connection, in the sense advantageous to our business; for though our circle of visiting acquaintance continued as limited as ever, we were now widely known in schools and families as teachers. When Frances had developed her plan, she intimated, in some closing sentences, her hopes for the future. If we only had good health and tolerable success, we might, she was sure, in time realize an independence, and that perhaps before we were too old to enjoy it. Then both she and I would rest; and what was to hinder us from going to live in England? England was still her Promised Land.
“她的计划是什么呢？”如果她想在事业上提升自己的话，下一步自然就由我们，或者至少她自己去攀登了。她提议创办一所学校。我们靠着自己的收入一直过得不错，只要勤俭节约，创办学校的启动资金还是有的。而且此时我们已经有了广阔的人际关系，从有利于事业的角度讲，也有资格办学校了；因为虽然我们所来往的交际圈子还是像之前一样窄，但是现在我们作为教师，在学校以及学生家庭那里已经广为人知。弗朗西斯详尽阐述完她的计划，最后几句话还透露出她对未来的希望。她确信，只要我们身体健康，诸事顺利，我们迟早会办成一所独立的学校，也许不用等到年老力衰时就能乐见其成。那时我们都可以休息了；到那时还有什么能够阻止我们一起到英国生活呢？英国仍然是她的理想圣地。
I put no obstacle in her way, raised no objection. I knew she was not one who could live quiescent and inactive, or even comparatively inactive. Duties she must have to fulfil, and important duties; work to do, and exciting, absorbing, profitable work. Strong faculties stirred in her frame, and they demanded full nourishment, free exercise. Mine was not the hand ever to starve or cramp them; no, I delighted in offering them sustenance, and in clearing them wider space for action.
我没有在她的前行道路上设置障碍，没有提出反对意见。我知道她不是个能够无所作为就活着的人，就算比较懒散也不行。她必须要完成一些任务，而且是重要的任务。她需要有工作做，而且是令人激动、引人入胜而且有益的工作。她的身上蕴含着强大的才能，它们需要充足的营养，并得到自由的发挥。我不会让它们挨饿或者束缚它们；不会的，我很乐意为它们提供支持，为它们拓宽施展才能的空间。
'You have conceived a plan, Frances,' said I, 'and a good plan; execute it. You have my free consent, and wherever and whenever my assistance is wanted, ask and you shall have.' 
“你已经构想出一个计划，弗朗西斯，”我说，“一个很好的计划；着手去实施吧。我绝对赞同，只要你需要我的帮助，我随时随地都会帮助你的。“
Frances' eyes thanked me almost with tears, just a sparkle or two, soon brushed away. She possessed herself of my hand too, and held it for some time very close clasped in both her own, but she said no more than 'Thank you, monsieur.' 
弗朗西斯的眼眶中几乎充满了感激的泪水，不过只是一两滴泪珠，瞬间便擦掉了。她也抓住我的手，双手紧紧地握了很久，但她只是说：“谢谢您，先生。”
We passed a divine day, and came home late, lighted by a full summer moon.
我们过了极其美好的一天，回家很晚，一轮夏日满月照亮了我们回家的路。
Ten years rushed now upon me with dusty, vibrating, unresting wings—years of bustle, action, unslacked endeavour; years in which I and my wife, having launched ourselves in the full career of progress, as progress whirls on in European capitals, scarcely knew repose, were strangers to amusement, never thought of indulgence, and yet, as our course ran side by side, as we marched hand in hand, we neither murmured, repented, nor faltered. Hope indeed cheered us; health kept us up; harmony of thought and deed smoothed many difficulties, and finally success bestowed every now and then encouraging reward on diligence. Our school became one of the most popular in Brussels, and as by degrees we raised our terms and elevated our system of education, our choice of pupils grew more select, and at length included the children of the best families in Belgium. We had too an excellent connection in England, first opened by the unsolicited recommendation of Mr. Hunsden, who having been over, and having abused me for my prosperity in set terms, went back, and soon after sent a leash of young —shire heiresses—his cousins; as he said, 'to be polished off by Mrs. Crimsworth.' 
如今十年时光已经带着满是灰尘的翅膀不断地扑闪着从我身边掠过——这是繁忙的十年，奋斗的十年，毫不松懈的十年；这十年来，我和妻子施尽全力推行着我们的计划，就像一连串欧洲各国首都的发展计划一样，我们几乎不知道休息，也从不纵情享乐；在我们肩并肩，手挽手的前进过程中，都从未抱怨、后悔或者踌躇过。希望确实激励着我们；健康支持着我们；想法与行动的一致克服了很多困难，最终成功不时对我们的勤劳给予鼓励性的回报。我们的学校成为布鲁塞尔最受欢迎的学校之一，随着我们逐步提高学费，改善教育体制，我们挑选学生也更加苛刻，甚至后来还有来自比利时最有名望的家族的孩子们。我们在英国也有了很好的渠道，首先是由亨斯登先生主动提出推荐而开辟的，他来过一次，臭骂我竟如此顺利地调整了学费，回去之后不久就送来了某某郡的三位女继承人——他的几位表妹；就像他说的，“让克里姆斯沃思夫人打磨一下。”
As to this same Mrs. Crimsworth, in one sense she was become another woman, though in another she remained unchanged. So different was she under different circumstances, I seemed to possess two wives. The faculties of her nature already disclosed when I married her, remained fresh and fair; but other faculties shot up strong, branched out broad, and quite altered the external character of the plant. Firmness activity, and enterprise covered with grave foliage poetic feeling and fervour; but these flowers were still there, preserved pure and dewy under the umbrage of later growth and hardier nature. Perhaps I only in the world knew the secret of their existence, but to me they were ever ready to yield an exquisite fragrance and present a beauty as chaste as radiant.
至于那位克里姆斯沃思夫人，在某个方面讲她已经变成另一个女人，尽管从另一个方面讲她还没有变。她在不同情况下简直迥然不同，我似乎有着两个妻子。她的天生才干在我和她结婚时便已展现，现在仍然朝气蓬勃，讨人喜爱；而其他方面的才能也在迅速成长，抽枝发芽，大大地改变了她原有的外在性格。之前的倔强好动积极上进，已被严肃的外表以及诗人般的感情和热情所掩盖；但是这些花儿依然生长在那里，在后来成长的有着更为坚强本性的树阴之中保持着当初的纯洁和雨露。也许在这世界上只有我知道它们的存在这个秘密，但是对于我，它们随时都散发着精美的芬芳，展现出光芒四射的高雅之美。
In the daytime my house and establishment were conducted by madame the directress, a stately and elegant woman, bearing much anxious thought on her large brow, much calculated dignity in her serious mien. Immediately after breakfast I used to part with this lady. I went to my college, she to her schoolroom. Returning for an hour in the course of the day, I found her always in class, intently occupied—silence, industry, observance attending on her presence. When not actually teaching, she was overlooking and guiding by eye and gesture; she then appeared vigilant and solicitous. When communicating instruction, her aspect was more animated; she seemed to feel a certain enjoyment in the occupation. The language in which she addressed her pupils, though simple and unpretending, was never trite or dry. She did not speak from routine formulas—she made her own phrases as she went on, and very nervous and impressive phrases they frequently were; often, when elucidating favourite points of history or geography, she would wax genuinely eloquent in her earnestness. Her pupils, or at least the elder and more intelligent amongst them, recognized well the language of a superior mind. They felt too, and some of them received the impression of elevated sentiments. There was little fondling between mistress and girls, but some of Frances' pupils in time learnt to love her sincerely, all of them beheld her with respect. Her general demeanour towards them was serious, sometimes benignant when they pleased her with their progress and attention, always scrupulously refined and considerate. In cases where reproof or punishment was called for she was usually forbearing enough; but if any took advantage of that forbearance, which sometimes happened, a sharp, sudden, and lightning-like severity taught the culprit the extent of the mistake committed. Sometimes a gleam of tenderness softened her eyes and manner, but this was rare—only when a pupil was sick, or when it pined after home, or in the case of some little motherless child, or of one much poorer than its companions, whose scanty wardrobe and mean appointments brought on it the contempt of the jewelled young countesses and silk-dad misses. Over such feeble fledglings the directress spread a wing of kindliest protection. It was to their bedsides she came at night to tuck them warmly in; it was after them she looked in winter to see that they always had a comfortable seat by the stove; it was they who by turns were summoned to the salon to receive some little dole of cake or fruit—to sit on a footstool at the fireside—to enjoy home comforts, and almost home liberty, for an evening together—to be spoken to gently and softly, comforted, encouraged, cherished, and, when bedtime came, dismissed with a kiss of true tenderness. As to Julia and Georgiana G-, daughters of an English baronet, as to Mdlle. Mathilde de-, heiress of a Belgian count, and sundry other children of patrician race, the directress was careful of them as of the others, anxious for their progress as for that of the rest; but it never seemed to enter her head to distinguish them by a mark of preference. One girl of noble blood she loved dearly—a young Irish baroness—Lady Catherine; but it was for her enthusiastic heart and clever head, for her generosity and her genius—the title and rank went for nothing.
在白天，我的家和学校都是由这位女校长夫人主管，她庄严而优雅，那宽大的额头上挂着很多思虑，严肃的神情中带有适当的尊严。一吃完早饭我便和这位夫人道别。我要去我的学校，她要去她的教室。我一天就回来一个小时，总是看见她在教室里一心一意地忙碌着——沉默寡言，孜孜不倦，极为细心。当她没有课时，也会监督着，用眼神和手势指导他人；那时她显得警惕而又细心。上课时，她的表情更为活泼，似乎在教书中找到了某种快乐。她对学生说话时语言简洁而不做作，从不会用陈词滥调。她不依常规说话，而是有一套自己的措辞，她说话时经常会出现一些强健有力而又发人深省的句子；讲述历史或者地理里她最爱的章节时，她便会滔滔不绝地认真讲。她的学生们，至少那些年长或者比较聪明的学生们，清楚地认识到她语言中体现的出众的思维。他们也感觉到了她的高尚品德，这给其中有些人留下深刻印象。这位老师并没有溺爱这些学生，但是弗朗西斯的一些学生们后来还是学会真心真意地爱上了她，所有学生都很尊敬她。她对她们的态度总的来说是严肃的，有时当她们取得进步或者认真听讲让她很高兴时，她的态度会和蔼一些，但她总是很谨慎地表现出优雅与体贴。有时当需要责备或者处罚学生时，她总是竭力忍耐；但是如果有人利用她的忍耐不知悔改——这情况时有发生，她就会像闪电一样突然变得严厉起来，让这样的学生知道他们犯的错误有多严重。有时她的眼睛和举止中会透露出一丝对学生的温和之情，但是那很少见，只有当某位学生生病了，或者想家了，或者面对某个没有妈妈的小孩，或者某个比其他同学都要穷的孩子，因为衣着简朴，用具简陋，而受到那些穿金戴银的年轻女伯爵或者衣着光鲜的小姐们的嘲笑时。遇到这些羽翼未丰的小鸟，女校长便会张开最仁慈的翅膀保护她们。晚上她会到她们的床边给她们掖一下被子，让她们睡得暖和；冬天她会照顾她们，让她们总能舒服地坐在火炉边；她把她们轮流叫到客厅里，给她们一些小蛋糕或者水果，让她们坐在火炉旁边的脚凳上，一起享受一晚家庭的温暖，甚至可以说家庭自由，她温和地跟她们说话，安慰她们，鼓励她们，珍视她们，到了睡觉时间就会温柔地亲吻她们，让她们回去睡觉。对于一位英国准男爵的的女儿茱莉亚和乔治亚娜，以及一位比利时伯爵的女继承人马蒂尔德·某某小姐，以及其他各种贵族小姐，女校长会像对待其他人一样关怀她们，像对待其他人一样渴望她们的进步；但是在她脑海中绝没有偏爱她们，对她们区分对待的想法。她非常喜爱其中一位贵族女孩，那是位年轻的爱尔兰女男爵，凯瑟琳小姐；但是那是因为她为人热心，又很聪明，慷慨大方，富有天分，与头衔和地位毫无关系。
My afternoons were spent also in college, with the exception of an hour that my wife daily exacted of me for her establishment, and with which she would not dispense. She said that I must spend that time amongst her pupils to learn their characters, to be au courant with everything that was passing in the house, to become interested in what interested her, to be able to give her my opinion on knotty points when she required it; and this she did constantly, never allowing my interest in the pupils to fall asleep, and never making any change of importance without my cognizance and consent. She delighted to sit by me when I gave my lessons (lessons in literature), her hands folded on her knee, the most fixedly attentive of any present. She rarely addressed me in class; when she did, it was with an air of marked deference. It was her pleasure, her joy, to make me still the master in all things.
我下午也在学校里度过，我妻子要求我每天去她学校一个小时，这一点她从不让步。她说我那段时间必须跟她的学生在一起，以便了解她们的性格，熟悉那栋房子里发生的一切，她感兴趣的我也要感兴趣，在她需要我的意见时我要能够在一些棘手的问题上提出自己的看法；她一直这样做着，绝不允许我对学生们的兴趣减少，在没有得到我的认定和同意之前，她从不会做任何重大的改变。当我讲课时（文学课），她喜欢坐在我旁边，双手叠放在膝盖上，比在场的其他人都要聚精会神。课堂上她很少和我说话；就是说话时也明显地带着恭敬的神色。她很乐意，也很高兴仍让我在一切事情上做主。
At six o'clock p.m. my daily labours ceased. I then came home, for my home was my heaven. Ever at that hour, as I entered our private sitting-room, the lady directress vanished from before my eyes, and Frances Henri, my own little lace-mender, was magically restored to my arms. Much disappointed she would have been if her master had not been as constant to the tryst as herself, and if his truthful kiss had not been prompt to answer her soft, 'Bon soir, monsieur.' 
下午六点钟，我一天的工作结束了。之后我会回到家里，因为家就是我的天堂。每次那个时候，当我走进我们的私人起居室时，女校长夫人就从我的眼前消失，我的小缝补花边女工弗朗西斯·亨利又奇迹般地回到我的怀中。如果她的老师没有像她自己那样持续赴约，如果当她温柔地说“晚上好，先生”时，她的老师没有立即报以真诚的一吻，那她就会很失望。
Talk French to me she would, and many a punishment she has had for her wilfulness. I fear the choice of chastisement must have been injudicious, for instead of correcting the fault, it seemed to encourage its renewal. Our evenings were our own; that recreation was necessary to refresh our strength for the due discharge of our duties. Sometimes we spent them all in conversation, and my young Genevese, now that she was thoroughly accustomed to her English professor, now that she loved him too absolutely to fear him much, reposed in him a confidence so unlimited that topics of conversation could no more be wanting with him than subjects for communion with her own heart. In those moments, happy as a bird with its mate, she would show me what she had of vivacity, of mirth, of originality in her well-dowered nature. She would show, too, some stores of raillery, of malice, and would vex, tease, pique me sometimes about what she called my bizarreries anglaises, my caprices insulaires, with a wild and witty wickedness that made a perfect white demon of her while it lasted. This was rare, however, and the elfish freak was always short. Sometimes when driven a little hard in the war of words—for her tongue did ample justice to the pith, the point, the delicacy of her native French, in which language she always attacked me—I used to turn upon her with my old decision, and arrest bodily the sprite that teased me. Vain idea! no sooner had I grasped hand or arm than the elf was gone; the provocative smile quenched in the expressive brown eyes, and a ray of gentle homage shone under the lids in its place. I had seized a mere vexing fairy, and found a submissive and supplicating little mortal woman in my arms. Then I made her get a look and read English to me for an hour by way of penance. I frequently dosed her with Wordsworth in this way, and Wordsworth steadied her soon. She had a difficulty in comprehending his deep, serene, and sober mind. His language, too, was not facile to her. She had to ask questions, to sue for explanations, to be like a child and a novice, and to acknowledge me as her senior and director. Her instinct instantly penetrated and possessed the meaning of more ardent and imaginative writers. Byron excited her; Scott she loved; Wordsworth only she puzzled at, wondered over, and hesitated to pronounce an opinion upon.
她总是对我说法语，因为她这固执我已经多次惩罚她。我觉得我对她的惩罚手段很不慎重，因为这不但没有改正她的缺点，却似乎还使她变本加厉。夜晚是属于我们自己的；此时的放松对于恢复我们的体力，应付第二天的工作很有必要。有时我们整个晚上都在谈话，我年轻的日内瓦女士现在因为已经完全适应了她的英国老师，因为她爱他爱得太深所以都没有对他心存畏惧了，她对他有着无限的信任，所以她和他已经是无所不谈，就好像和自己的心灵交流一样自由。那时，她就会像和同伴在一起的小鸟一样幸福，她会向我展示她那优良天性里的活泼、愉悦和奇特之处。她也会展示她暗藏的逗趣与调皮的品质，有时她会称呼包括我在内的英国人为“古怪的英国佬”和“反复无常的岛民”来激怒我、嘲笑我、刺伤我的自尊心，她说这些话时带着一种狂野而又诙谐的邪恶，活生生地像一个无害的小恶魔。但是这很少发生，而且这种精灵般捣蛋行径也总是很短暂。她总是用法语攻击我，因为她的舌头能够将她母语的精髓、要点和精致表现到极致，有时在论战中我词穷时，会以一贯的果断对她展开反攻，伸手去抓这个戏弄我的小精灵。可这毫无用处！我一抓到她的手或者胳膊，这个小精灵就不见了；那挑衅似的微笑在她深情的褐色眼睛中消失了，取而代之的是出现在眼睑下温和而尊敬的光芒。我抓住的只是一个让人苦恼的小仙女，怀中抱的是一个柔顺、乞求宽恕的人间小女人。那时我就会让她拿出一本书，读一个小时的英语给我听，以做惩罚。我常常通过这种方式让她读华兹华斯的诗，因为他很快就会让她安静下来。她很难理解华兹华斯那深奥、平静而审慎的思想。他的语言对她来说也不容易懂。她只得问问题，寻求解释，就像一个小孩，像个生手一样，不得不承认我是她的长者和老师。她的天分使她立即就能洞察并且掌握那些思想更激烈又更富有想象力的作家的作品涵义。拜伦使她激动，司各特令她爱慕，只有华兹华斯让她既十分迷惑又很惊叹，并且不敢对他妄加评述。
But whether she read to me or talked with me; whether she teased me in French or entreated me in English; whether she jested with wit or inquired with deference, narrated with interest or listened with attention; whether she smiled at me or on me, always at nine o'clock I was left—abandoned. She would extricate herself from my arms, quit my side, take her lamp, and be gone. Her mission was upstairs. I have followed her sometimes and watched her. First she opened the door of the dortior (the pupils' chamber), noiselessly she glided up the long room between the two rows of white beds, surveyed all the sleepers. If any were wakeful, especially if any were sad spoke to them and soothed them, stood some minutes to ascertain that all was safe and tranquil, trimmed the watchlight which burned in the apartment all night, then withdrew closing the door behind her without sound. Thence she glided to our own chamber. It had a little cabinet within. This she sought. There, too, appeared a bed but one, and that a very small one; her face (the night I followed and observed her) changed as she approached this tiny couch. From grave it warmed to earnest; she shaded with one hand the lamp she held in the other; she bent above the pillow and hung over a child asleep. Its slumber (that evening, at least, and usually, I believe) was sound and calm; no tear wet its dark eyelashes; no fever heated its round cheek; no ill dream discomposed its budding features. Frances gazed; she did not smile, and yet the deepest delight filled, flushed her face. Feeling, pleasurable, powerful, worked in her whole frame, which still was motionless. I saw, indeed, her heart heave, her lips were a little apart, her breathing grew somewhat hurried. The child smiled; then at last the mother smiled too, and said in low soliloquy, 'God bless my little son!' She stooped closer over him, breathed the softest of kisses on his brow, covered his minute hand with hers, and at last started up and came away. I regained the parlour before her. Entering it two minutes later, she said quietly as she put down her extinguished lamp,— 
但是不管她给我读书还是陪我说话，不管她用法语嘲笑我还是用英语哀求我；不管她用小聪明打趣我还是顺从地询问我；不管她饶有兴趣地向我诉说，还是聚精会神地倾听着我说话；不管她嘲笑我还是赞许我，九点的时候她就丢下我——抛弃我。她会从我的手臂中挣脱开，离开我身边，提着灯消失不见。她的任务就在楼上。有时我会跟踪并观察她。她先打开宿舍的门（学生宿舍），从两排长长的白色床边悄悄走过，查看所有睡觉的学生。如果有人还是醒的，尤其是有人在伤心的话，她就会跟她们说话，安慰她们，站在那儿几分钟以确定所有的人都安然无事，之后便调调公寓里那个整夜都亮着的照明灯，然后无声无息地离开宿舍并随手关上宿舍门。然后她又溜回我们自己的房间。房间里有个小房间。她摸进里面。那里面也有一张床，一张很小的床；她一靠近那个小沙发床表情就变了（那晚我跟踪她，观察着她）。表情由严肃变成温和与诚挚；她一手遮住另一只手上提着的灯；身子弯向枕边熟睡的婴儿。那小孩睡得又十分香甜（至少那晚是那样，我相信平时也是那样）；没有眼泪打湿他黑色的眼睫毛；没有发热染红他圆圆的脸颊；没有噩梦打搅他如花蕾般的面容。弗朗西斯注视着他，她没有笑，但是内心里充斥着深切的欢喜，她的脸上泛起了红晕。一种快乐又强烈的感情在仍一动不动的全身上下翻动着。我的确看见她的心脏起伏，嘴唇微张，呼吸有些加快。孩子笑了，最终母亲也笑了，轻声地自言自语道，“上帝保佑我的小儿子！”她又弯了弯腰靠他更近些，用最温柔的吻吻着他的额头，用她的手盖住他的小手，最终站起身离去。我在她之前回到了客厅。两分钟之后她进来了，一边放下已经灭了的灯一边轻轻地说：
'Victor rests well. He smiled in his sleep. He has your smile, monsieur.' 
“维克托睡得好着呢。睡的时候还笑。他的笑容跟您的一样，先生。”
The said Victor was, of course, her own boy, born in the third year of our marriage. His Christian name had been given him in honour of M. Vandenhuten, who continued always our trusty and well-beloved friend.
这里的维克托当然是她的亲生儿子，是在我们婚后第三年出生的。给他取这个洗礼名是为了表示对范登赫顿先生的敬意，范登赫顿先生一直都是我们值得信赖和尊敬的朋友。
Frances was, then, a good and dear wife to me, because I was to her a good, just, and faithful husband. What she would have been had she married a harsh, envious, careless man—a profligate, a prodigal, a drunkard, or a tyrant—is another question, and one which I once propounded to her. Her answer, given after some reflection, was,— 
那时弗朗西斯是我亲爱的好妻子，因为我是她正直而忠实的好丈夫。假如她嫁给了一个苛刻无情，嫉妒好胜又淡漠随便的男人——一个放荡之人，一个挥金如土之人，一个酒鬼或者一个专制的人，她会怎么样——那就是另一种情况了，并且这是我曾经向她提出过的一个问题。她想了片刻后回答道：
'I should have tried to endure the evil or cure it for a while; and when I found it intolerable and incurable, I should have left my torturer suddenly and silently.' 
“我会试图忍受那个魔鬼一段时间，或者帮助他改正缺点；如果我发现那确实无法忍受并且无可救药后，我会悄悄地突然离开那个折磨我的人。”
'And if law or might had forced you back again?' 
“如果法律或者强权又强迫你回到他的身边呢？”
'What! to a drunkard, a profligate, a selfish spendthrift, an unjust fool?' 
“什么！回到一个酒鬼，一个放荡之人，一个自私的挥金如土的人，一个不正直的傻瓜身边？”
'Yes.' 
“是啊。”
'I would have gone back, again assured myself whether or not his vice and my misery were capable of remedy, and if not, have left him again.' 
“那我会再回到他身边，再次弄清楚他的罪恶是否能得救，我的痛苦是否能平复，如果不能的话，我会再次离开他。”
'And if again forced to return and compelled to abide?' 
“假如再次逼迫你回到他身边，强迫你忍受呢？”
'I don't know,' she said hastily. 'Why do you ask me, monsieur?' 
“我不知道，”她匆匆地回答说，“你为什么问我这个呢，先生？”
I would have an answer, because I saw a strange kind of spirit in her eye, whose voice I determined to waken.
我想要一个答案，因为我看见她眼睛里有一种奇怪的幽灵，我想唤醒那个幽灵的声音。
'Monsieur, if a wife's nature loathes that of the man she is wedded to, marriage must he slavery. Against slavery all right thinkers revolt, and though torture be the price of resistance, torture must be dared. Though the only road to freedom lie through the gates of death, those gates must be passed, for freedom is indispensable. Then, monsieur, I would resist as far as my strength permitted; when that strength failed I should be sure of a refuge. Death would certainly screen me both from bad laws and their consequences.
“先生，如果一个妻子与她所嫁的人本性不和，那他们的婚姻肯定就是一种奴隶制。所有正直的思想家都会反对奴隶制，虽然反抗的代价可能是痛苦折磨，但是要敢于直面折磨。尽管通往自由的唯一之路是穿过死亡之门，但是那些门是必须要穿过的，因为自由是不可或缺的。那么，先生，我会用尽全力去反抗；当我心力交瘁时，我就会找到避难之所。死亡肯定可以使我逃脱不正义的法律和这种法律所带来的恶果。”
'Voluntary death, Frances?' 
“自愿去死吗，弗朗西斯？”
'No, monsieur. I'd have courage to live out every throe of anguish fate assigned me, and principle to contend for justice and liberty to the last.' 
“不是的，先生。我要有勇气撑过命运安排给我的每份痛苦，力争为正义和自由斗争到底。“
'I see you would have made no patient Grizzle. And now, supposing fate had merely assigned you the lot of an old maid, what then? How would you have liked celibacy?' 
“我看你是做不成一位耐心的老太太的。现在假使命运只安排你做一位老处女，那你怎么办？你会喜欢那种独身生活吗？”
'Not much, certainly. An old maid's life must doubtless be void and vapid, her heart strained and empty. Had I been an old maid, I should have spent existence in efforts to fill the void and ease the aching. I should have probably failed, and died weary and disappointed, despised and of no account, like other single women. But I'm not an old maid,' she added quickly, 'I should have been, though, but for my master. I should never have suited any man but Professor Crimsworth—no other gentleman, French, English, or Belgian, would have thought me amiable or handsome; and I doubt whether I should have cared for the approbation of many others, if I could have obtained it. Now, I have been Professor Crimsworth's wife eight years, and what is he in my eyes? Is he honourable, beloved—' She stopped; her voice was cut off, her eyes suddenly suffused. She and I were standing side by side. She threw her arms round me, and strained me to her heart with passionate earnestness. The energy of her whole being glowed in her dark and then dilated eye, and crimsoned her animated cheek. Her look and movement were like inspiration; in one there was such a flash, in the other such a power. Half an hour afterwards, when she had become calm I asked where all that wild vigour was gone which had transformed her erewhile and made her glance so thrilling and ardent, her action so rapid and strong. She looked down, smiling softly and passively.
“当然不喜欢了。老处女的生活肯定是空虚单调的，她的心灵因受压抑而空虚。如果我是一位老处女，我会终生努力去填充那种空虚，减少痛楚。我很有可能会失败，像其他单身女人一样劳累而失望地死去，受人鄙视，没有价值。但我不是一位老处女，”她很快地补上一句,“尽管要不是因为我的老师，我可能也是了。除了克里姆斯沃思老师外我不会钟意任何男人——没有任何绅士，不论是法国人，英国人还是比利时人，会认为我可爱美丽；如果他们对我表示认可的话，我想我也会不屑一顾的，现在我已经当了八年的克里姆斯沃思老师的妻子，他在我的眼里是什么样子的呢？他正直吗，值得人爱吗——”她停住了；她的声音突然停止，眼睛突然充满泪水。我们正肩并肩地站着。她突然抱住了我，热情而真诚地将我紧紧抱在她的胸前。她全身的能量都在那睁大的深色的眼睛中燃烧，映红了她那充满生气的脸颊。她的表情和动作就像是得到了神的指示；一个闪闪发光，一个充满力量。半个小时之后，当她冷静下来，我问她那狂野的激情去哪儿了，那激情刚刚使她顷刻间变了一个人，并使她的目光如此勾人魂魄和热切，她的动作如此快速而有力。她低下头，温和而勉强地一笑。
'I cannot tell where it is gone, monsieur,' said she,'but I know that, whenever it is wanted, it will come back again.' 
“我也不知道它去哪儿了，先生，”她说，“但是我知道只要有需要的话，它就会再回来。”
Behold us now at the close of the ten years, and we have realized an independence. The rapidity with which we attained this end had its origin in three reasons. Firstly, we worked so hard for it; secondly, we had no encumbrances to delay success; thirdly, as soon as we had capital to invest, two well-skilled counsellors, one in Belgium, one in England—namely, Vandenhuten and Hunsden—gave us each a word of advice as to the sort of investment to be chosen. The suggestion made was judicious; and, being promptly acted on, the result proved gainful—I need not say how gainful. I communicated details to Messrs. Vandenhuten and Hunsden. Nobody else can be interested in hearing them.
现在仔细看看十年后的我们吧，我们已经建立了我们当初所希望的独立学校。我们能这么快获得这样的结局有三个原因。第一，我们为此很努力地工作；第二，我们没碰到什么推迟成功的阻碍；第三，我们一有资金要投资，就有两位高深的顾问，一位在比利时，一位在英国——就是范登赫顿和亨斯登——各自在选择哪种投资方面给我们建议。他们的建议都是明智的，而且能够立即见效，结果都很有收益——至于具体收益多少就不用我说了吧。这种详细情况我只会对范登赫顿先生和亨斯登先生讲述。其他人都不会对此感兴趣的。
Accounts being wound up, and our professional connection disposed of, we both agreed that, as Mammon was not our master, nor his service that in which we desired to spend our lives, as our desires were temperate, and our habits unostentatious, we had now abundance to live on, abundance to leave our boy, and should besides always have a balance on hand which, properly managed by right sympathy and unselfish activity, might help Philanthropy in her enterprises, and put solace into the hand of Charity.
账目已经算清，我们的业务联系也已经处理完，我们一致认为，财神不是我们的主人，我们也不愿意一辈子为它打工，因为我们的追求也并不高，我们的生活习惯也很朴素，我们现在有足够的钱活下去，也有足够的钱留给我们的儿子，除此之外手头上还会有些余钱，如果能够通过正义的同情心以及无私的捐助来合理地处理这些钱的话，它们能够用来帮助慈善事业，并给予贫民以安慰。
To England we now resolved to take wing. We arrived there safely. Frances realized the dream of her lifetime. We spent a whole summer and autumn in travelling from end to end of the British Islands, and afterwards passed a winter in London. Then we thought it high time to fix our residence. My heart yearned towards my native county of —shire; and it is in —shire I now live; it is in the library of my own home I am now writing. That home lies amid a sequestered and rather hilly region, thirty miles removed from X—; a region whose verdure the smoke of mills has not yet sullied, whose waters still rim pure, whose swells of moorland preserve in some ferny glens that lie between them the very primal wildness of nature, her moss, her bracken, her blue-bells, her scents of reed and heather, her free and fresh breezes. My house is a picturesque and not too spacious dwelling, with low and long windows, a trellised and leaf-veiled porch over the front door, just now, on this summer evening, looking like an arch of roses and ivy. The garden is chiefly laid out in lawn, formed of the sod of the hills, with herbage short and soft as moss, full of its own peculiar flowers, tiny and starlike, embedded in the minute embroidery of their fine foliage. At the bottom of the sloping garden there is a wicket, which opens upon a lane as green as the lawn, very long, shady, and little frequented. On the turf of this lane generally appear the first daisies of spring—whence its name, Daisy Lane, serving also as a distinction to the house.
如今我们下定决心展翅飞向英国。我们安全地到达了那里。弗朗西斯实现了她毕生的梦想。我们花了一整个夏天和秋天的时间玩遍了整个不列颠岛，随后又在伦敦度过了一个冬天。后来我们认为确实该找个地方定居下来了。我心里思念着我在某某郡的故乡，我们如今就在那个郡里住下来了；此时此刻我就在自己家的书房里写作。那个家位于一个偏僻幽静又多山的地区，离X镇三十里远；那里碧绿的山坡还没有被工厂排放的浓烟玷污，那里的水依然清澈纯净，那里长满蕨类的幽谷有着很多隆起的沼泽地，保持着自然界最为原始的荒凉景象，满地的苔藓，满地的欧洲厥，满地的风铃草，弥散着芦苇与石楠花的芬芳，吹荡着自由清新的微风。我的房子风景如画，并不算宽敞，有着低而长的窗户，前门前面有一个由格架搭成的门廊，上面覆盖着绿叶，在这个夏天的夜晚，看起来就像一个用玫瑰和常春藤搭成的拱门。花园主要是草坪，由山上的草皮铺盖而成，里面生长着像苔藓一样矮而柔软的植物，各自开满了奇特的鲜花，星星般的小花儿点缀在如刺绣般精致的绿叶中。斜坡花园的底下有一扇小门，打开便是一条如草坪般碧绿的小径，又长又阴凉，很幽僻。春天时这条小径的草皮上一般会长出最早的雏菊，小路因此得名——雏菊路，这也是这座房子的一大特色。
It terminates (the lane I mean) in a valley full of wood; which wood—chiefly oak and beech—spreads shadowy about the vicinage of a very old mansion, one of the Flizabethan structures, much larger, as well as more antique than Daisy Lane, the property and residence of an individual familiar both to me and to the reader. Yes, in Hunsden Wood—for so are those glades and that gray building, with many gables and more chimneys, named—abides Yorke Hunsden, still unmarried, never, I suppose, having yet found his ideal, though I know at least a score of young ladies within a circuit of forty miles who would be willing to assist him in the search.
它（我指的是那条小径）一直延伸至一个长满树木的峡谷；那片树林——主要是橡树和山毛榉——葱葱郁郁，伸展到一栋很古老的房子附近，那是一栋伊丽莎白式建筑，比起雏菊路的房子大得多也古老得多，这里的财产和房子都属于一个我和读者都很熟悉的人。是的，在亨斯登树林里——因为那些林间空地和那栋有很多山形墙与更多烟囱的灰色建筑都在树林里，——那里住着约克·亨斯登，他依然单身，我想是还没有找到他理想的女人，虽然据我所知，在方圆四十英里内少说也有二十多个年轻女士愿意帮助他寻找妻子。
The estate fell to him by the death of his father, five years since. He has given up trade, after having made by it sufficient to pay off some encumbrances by which the family heritage was burdened. I say he abides here, but I do not think he is resident above five months out of the twelve. He wanders from land to land, and spends some part of each winter in town. He frequently brings visitors with him when he comes to —shire, and these visitors are often foreigners. Sometimes he has a German metaphysician, sometimes a French savant. He had once a dissatisfied and savage-looking Italian, who neither sang nor played, and of whom Frances affirmed that he had 'tout l'air d' un conspirateur.' 
这些财产是五年前他父亲去世时留给他的。在还清用家产抵押的债务之后，他就放弃了商业。我说他住在那里，但是我认为一年十二个月他不会在这儿住超过五个月。他四处游玩，每年冬天有段时间住在城里。每次他来某某郡时总是会带客人来，这些客人经常都是外国人。有时会带一个德国玄学家，有时是一个法国学者。有次他带了一个面露不满、长相野蛮的意大利人，他既不唱也不玩，弗朗西斯断定他有着“一副十足的阴谋家相”。
What English guests Hunsden invites are all either men of Birmingham or Manchester—hard men, seemingly knit up in one thought, whose talk is of free trade. The foreign visitors, too, are politicians. They take a wider theme—European progress—the spread of liberal sentiments over the Continent; on their mental tablets, the names of Russia, Austria, and the Pope are inscribed in red ink. I have heard some of them talk vigorous sense—yea, I have been present at polyglot discussions in the old, oak-lined dining-room at Hunsden Wood, where a singular insight was given of the sentiments entertained by resolute minds respecting old northern despotisms and older southern superstitions; also, I have heard much twaddle, enounced chiefly in French and Deutsch, but let that pass. Hunsden himself tolerated the drivelling theorists; with the practical men he seemed leagued hand and heart.
亨斯登请来的英国客人都是伯明翰人或者曼彻斯特人，这些人一副严肃的样子，而且，他们彷佛是由同一种思想串在一起似的，谈话内容都是关于自由贸易的。这些外国访客也都是政治家。他们谈话的主题比较宽泛，像欧洲的进步、自由主义思想在大陆的传播；在他们内心的写字板里，俄国、奥地利和罗马教皇的名字都是用红笔标刻的。我曾听见他们中一些人很有激情地谈论——是的，我曾在亨斯登树林古老的、用橡木装饰的餐厅里亲耳听见他们用多国语言进行讨论，当谈及尊重古老北方的独裁专制和更为古老的南方的迷信行为时，他们这些果敢的人有着一些独特的见解；我还听了他们主要用法语和德语说的许多瞎扯的话，关于这些我就不多说了。亨斯登本人容忍了这些满口胡言乱语的理论家；他似乎与实用主义者是同心协力的。
When Hunsden is staying alone at the Wood (which seldom happens) he generally find his way two or three times a week to Daisy Lane. He has a philanthropic motive for coming to smoke his cigar in our porch on summer evenings. He says he does it to kill the earwigs amongst the roses, with which insects, but for his benevolent fumigations, he intimates we should certainly be overrun. On wet days, too, we are almost sure to see him. According to him, it gets on time to work me into lunacy by treading on my mental corns, or to force from Mrs. Crimsworth revelations of the dragon within her by insulting the memory of Hofer and Tell.
当亨斯登一个人在林子里时（他很少这样做），一般每星期来雏菊路两三次。夏天的晚上，他出于一个善意的动机来我们家门廊下抽烟。他说这样做是为了杀死玫瑰丛里的蜈蚣，他暗示说要不是他善意的烟熏法，我们肯定会发蜈蚣灾。每当下雨天时我们也几乎肯定能见到他。据他所说，是时候践踏我内心的要害而让我发狂，或者是通过侮辱霍弗和特尔把克里姆斯沃思夫人心中的恶龙逗出来。
We also go frequently to Hunsden Wood, and both I and Frances relish a visit there highly. If there are other guests, their characters are an interesting study; their conversation is exciting and strange. The absence of all local narrowness both in the host and his chosen society gives a metropolitan, almost a cosmopolitan, freedom and largeness to the talk. Hunsden himself is a polite man in his own house. He has, when he chooses to employ it, an inexhaustible power of entertaining guests. His very mansion, too, is interesting; the rooms look storied, the passages legendary, the low-ceiled chambers with their long rows of diamond-paned lattices have an old-world, haunted air. In his travels he has collected store of articles of vertu, which are well and tastefully disposed in his panelled or tapestried rooms. I have seen there one or two pictures and one or two pieces of statuary which many an aristocratic connoisseur might have envied.
我们也经常去亨斯登树林，我和弗朗西斯都很喜欢去那儿拜访他。如果有其他客人的话，我们就观察他们的性格，这很有趣；他们的对话令我们很激动，也很惊奇。无论是主人还是他挑选的朋友都不会局限于谈论乡土民情，而是谈论大都市，几乎是全世界，自由自在，无所不谈。亨斯登在自己的家中礼貌有加。只要他想，他就会有无穷的法宝来招待客人。他的府邸也很有趣；房间古色古香，走廊像是充满了历史气息，房间的天花板很低，有着一排排长长的亮闪闪的玻璃格子窗，给人一种古代的阴森的、鬼魂经常出没的感觉。他在外旅行时收集了很多古董，都很整齐而雅致地摆放在那镶着板的或者挂着花毯的房间里。我在那里见过一两张画像和一两件雕塑，这都使很多贵族鉴赏家艳羡不已。
When I and Frances have dined and spent an evening with Hunsden, he often walks home with us. His wood is large, and some of the timber is old and of huge growth. There are winding ways in it which, pursued through glade and brake, make the walk back to Daisy Lane a somewhat long one. Many a time, when we have had the benefit of a full moon, and when the night has been mild and balmy, when, moreover, a certain nightingale has been singing, and a certain stream, bid in alders, has lent the song a soft accompaniment, the remote church-bell of the one hamlet in a district of ten miles has tolled midnight ere the lord of the wood left us at our porch. Free-flowing was his talk at such hours, and far more quiet and gentle than in the daytime and before numbers. He would then forget politics and discussion, and would dwell on the past times of his house, on his family history, on himself and his own feelings—subjects each and all invested with a peculiar zest, for they were each and all unique. One glorious night in June, after I had been taunting him about his ideal bride, and asking him when she would come and graft her foreign beauty on the old Hunsden oak, he answered suddenly,— 
当我和弗朗西斯在亨斯登家吃过饭，度过一个晚上时光之后，他经常会步行送我们回家。他的树林很大，一些树木颇有年月，并且十分高耸。林中有着蜿蜒盘旋的小路，时而穿过空地，时而穿过灌木丛，使得返回雏菊路的道路变得有些长。很多次恰逢满月当空，夜色温和宜人，此时夜莺一展歌喉，隐于楷木中的泉水温柔地伴奏，直到从十英里之外某个小村隐约传来子夜教堂钟声时，树林的主人才会离开我们家的门廊。在这期间，他的谈话很随意，比在白天或者在人多的时候要平静很多，也要温和很多。彼时他就会忘记议论政治，会给我们详述他家的过去，讲述他家族的历史，讲述他自己和他自己的情感——这每一件事他都饶有兴趣，因为每一件事都是独一无二的。六月一个月光皎洁的夜晚，我嘲弄他关于他空想的新娘的事情，问他她何时才会将她那异质的美丽嫁接到古老的亨斯登橡树上，他突然回答说：
'You call her ideal; but see, here is her shadow, and there cannot be a shadow without a substance.' 
“你说她是空想的，但是你看，这是她的影子，没有实物的话就不会有影子。”
He had led us from the depth of the 'winding way' into a glade from whence the beeches withdrew, leaving it open to the sky. An unclouded moon poured her light into this glade and Hunsden held out under her beam an ivory miniature.
他带我们走出“蜿蜒小道”，来到一片山毛榉簇拥之外的空地，抬头就可望见天空。皓月洒下一片光辉到这片空地上，在月光下，亨斯登拿出一个象牙的小画像。
Frances, with eagerness, examined it first; then she gave it to me—still, however, pushing her little face close to mine, and seeking in my eyes what I thought of the portrait. I thought it represented a very handsome and very individual-looking female face, with, as he had once said, 'straight and harmonious features.' It was dark; the hair, raven-black, swept not only from the brow, but from the temples—seemed thrust away carelessly, as if such beauty dispensed with, nay, despised arrangement. The Italian eye looked straight into you, and an independent, determined eye it was; the mouth was as firm as fine, the chin ditto. On the back of the miniature was gilded 'Lucia.' 
弗朗西斯迫不及待地先看了看；然后交给我——但她的小脸蛋还是靠我很近，想从我的眼神中看出我对这幅画像是怎么看的。我觉得这是一张非常漂亮又很有个性的女性脸蛋，就像他之前说过那样，“相貌端正而匀称”。图像很暗；头发乌黑，既没有遮住额头，也没有盖住双鬓——似乎是随意地向后弄去，好像这种美女不需要，不，应该说是不屑于打扮。那双意大利式的眼睛直视着你，透着自由而执着的眼神，嘴唇坚毅而美丽，下巴亦然。画像的背面镀着金字“露西亚”。
'That is a real head,' was my conclusion.
“那是真人画像。”我得出结论。
Hunsden smiled.
亨斯登笑了笑。
'I think so,' he replied. 'All was real in Lucia.' 
“我想也是的。”他回答说。“露西亚的一切都是真实的。”
'And she was somebody you would have liked to marry, but could not?' 
“她就是你想娶却娶不了的女人？”
'I should certainly have liked to marry her, and that I have not done so is a proof that I could not.' 
“我肯定想娶她，但我没有娶她，说明我娶不了。”
He repossessed himself of the miniature, now again in Frances' hand, and put it away.
他要回现在在弗朗西斯手中的小画像，又收了起来。
'What do you think of it?' he asked of my wife, as he buttoned his coat over it.
“你觉得怎么样？”他边系大衣的纽扣将它揣起来边问我的妻子。
'I am sure Lucia once wore chains and broke them,' was the strange answer. 'I do not mean matrimonial chains,' she added, correcting herself, as if she feared misinterpretation,'but social chains of some sort. The face is that of one who has made an effort, and a successful and triumphant effort, to wrest some vigorous and valued faculty from insupportable constraint; and when Lucia's faculty got free, I am certain it spread wide pinions and carried her higher than—' She hesitated.
“我确定露西亚曾经戴过锁链又挣脱了它们，”她奇怪的回答道。“我指的并不是婚姻的锁链，”她又加以改正，似乎是担心他会理解错误，补充道，“而是某种社会的枷锁。她脸上的表情说明她一直在努力挣脱无法忍受的束缚，为了获得一种有力而宝贵的能力，而且她的努力成功了，获得了胜利，一旦露西亚的这种能力得到自由，我肯定它会展开双翅，将露西亚带上高空，高于——”。她犹豫了一下。
'Than what?' demanded Hunsden.
“高于什么东西？”亨斯登问道。
'Than les convenances permitted you to follow.' 
“高于你所追随的‘得体’，让你无法得到。”
'I think you grow spiteful—impertinent.' 
“我觉得你开始心怀恶意——说的话不着调。”
'Lucia has trodden the stage,' continued Frances. 'You never seriously thought of marrying her. You admired her originality, her fearlessness, her energy of body and mind; you delighted in her talent, whatever that was, whether song, dance, or dramatic representation; you worshipped her beauty, which was of the sort after your own heart; but I am sure she filled a sphere from whence you would never have thought of taking a wife.' 
“露西亚已经踏上舞台，”弗朗西斯继续说道，“你从来没有认真考虑要娶她。你欣赏她的独特，她的无畏，她身心的活力；你喜欢她的才能，无论是哪个方面，不管是唱歌，跳舞还是戏剧表演；你爱慕她的美貌，那是你内心所追求的那种；但是我确定你是不会在她的那个阶层里找个妻子的。”
'Ingenious,' remarked Hunsden; 'whether true or not is another question. Meantime, don't you feel your little lamp of a spirit wax very pale beside such a girandole as Lucia's?' 
“说的很好，”亨斯登评价说，“但至于说得对不对就要另当别论了。同时，在露西亚这样一个烛台旁边，你难道不认为你这个小小的精神烛光太暗淡了吗？”
'Yes.' 
“是的。”
'Candid, at least; and the professor will soon be dissatisfied with the dim light you give?' 
“你至少很坦率；教师先生很快就会对你所能发出的微弱光满感到不满的。”
'Will you, monsieur?' 
“您会这样吗，先生？”
'My sight was always too weak to endure a blaze, Frances.' And we had now reached the wicket.

Victor is as little of a pretty child as I am of a handsome man, or his mother of a fine woman. He is pale and spare, with large eyes, as dark as those of Frances, and as deeply set as mine. His shape is symmetrical enough, but slight; his health is good. I never saw a child smile less than he does, nor one who knits such a formidable brow when sitting over a book that interests him, or while listening to tales of adventure, peril, or wonder, narrated by his mother, Hunsden, or myself. But though still, he is not unhappy; though serious, not morose. He has a susceptibility to pleasurable sensations almost too keen, for it amounts to enthusiasm. He learned to read in the old-fashioned way out of a spelling-book at his mother's knee; and as he got on without driving by that method, she thought it unnecessary to buy him ivory letters, or to try any of the other inducements to learning now deemed indispensable. When he could read, he became a glutton of books, and is so still. His toys have been few, and he has never wanted more. For those he possesses he seems to have contracted a partiality amounting to affection. This feeling, directed towards one or two living animals of the house, strengthens almost to a passion.
维克托不是一个漂亮的小孩，就像我不是一个英俊男人，他的母亲也不是一个标致的女人一般。他脸色苍白，身体瘦小，有着大大的眼睛，像弗朗西斯的眼睛一样黑，像我的眼窝一样深。他的身材很匀称，但是有些瘦小，还算健康。我从没见过一个小孩比他笑得还少，也没有谁像他那样坐着看一本有趣的书，或者听他母亲、亨斯登或者我给他讲冒险、惊险或者神话故事时，眉头还那样紧皱的。尽管比较沉默，他并不是不快乐；虽然表情严肃但也并不孤僻。他很容易受欢乐情绪影响，常常反应过激，几乎有些痴狂了。他是通过很老式的方式，从放在妈妈膝盖上的拼音书开始学习识字的；因为后来他不用靠那种方法鞭策也能读下去时，弗朗西斯认为没有必要给他买象牙字母，也没有尝试那些现在被认为在刺激小孩读书方面是必不可少的方法。从他会读书开始，他便成了小书虫，现在仍然如此。他的玩具很少，他也从没要求要更多。他似乎对已拥有的玩具很偏爱，几乎到了钟情的程度。他的这种情感也延伸到家里养的一两只动物上，他对它们几乎到了深爱的地步。
Mr. Hunsden gave him a mastiff cub, which he called Yorke, after the donor. It grew to a superb dog, whose fierceness, however, was' much modified by the companionship and caresses of its young master. He would go nowhere, do nothing without Yorke. Yorke lay at his feet while he learned his lessons, played with him in the garden, walked with him in the lane and wood, sat near his chair at meals, was fed always by his own hand, was the first thing he sought in the morning, the last he left at night. Yorke accompanied Mr. Hunsden one day to X—, and was bitten in the street by a dog in a rabid state. As soon as Hunsden had brought him home, and had informed me of the circumstance, I went into the yard and shot him where he lay licking his wound. He was dead in an instant. He had not seen me level the gun; I stood behind him. I had scarcely been ten minutes in the house, when my ear was struck with sounds of anguish. I repaired to the yard once more, for they proceeded thence. Victor was kneeling beside his dead mastiff, bent over it, embracing its bull-like neck, and lost in a passion of the wildest woe. 亨斯登先生送给他一个小猛犬，他就依着赠送者的名字叫它约克。它后来长成一只威武的猛犬，但是在小主人的陪伴与爱抚下，它的凶猛大大地弱化了。没有约克陪伴，维克托哪儿都不去，什么都不干。学习功课的时候约克就躺在他的脚边，约克还和他在花园里嬉戏，陪他小径和树林里散步，吃饭时蹲在他的椅边，是由他亲手喂，他早上的第一件事就是找到约克，晚上睡觉前总是最后一个离开它。有一天约克陪着亨斯登先生去X镇，被街上一条患狂犬病的狗咬伤了。亨斯登先生一把它带回家，告知我情况之后，我马上到院子里开枪射死它，当时约克正在那儿卧着舔伤口。它当场死亡。它都没有看见我拿起枪，我站在它的后面。我回到屋里还没有十分钟，耳边便传来痛苦的哭声。我又回到院子里，因为哭声是从那边传来的。维克托跪在他死去的猛犬旁，弯下身子，抱着它如公牛脖子般的粗脖子，沉浸在悲恸之中。他看见了我。

他整个身子扑到那毫无知觉的尸体上。我耐心地等了很久，直到他已经哭得筋疲力尽；然后我把他抱到怀里交给他母亲，相信她能给他最好的安慰。她已经从窗户里看到整个情形。她没有出来是因为担心她的情绪会增加我的困难，但是她现在已经做好准备来安抚儿子。她把他抱入自己关爱的怀抱里，让他坐在她柔软的腿上；只是用她的双唇，她的眼睛还有她温柔的环抱安慰了他一段时间；当他的啜泣声慢慢停止，她便告诉他约克在死的时候没有任何痛苦，但是如果让它留下来，自然死去，结局会很可怕；她特别告诉他我并不残忍（因为那个想法似乎给可怜的维克托带来极大的痛苦），就是因为我爱约克和他，我才会那么做的，而且我现在看到他哭得那么厉害，几乎心都碎了。
Victor would have been no true son of his father had these considerations, these reasons, breathed in so low, so sweet a tone, married to caresses so benign, so tender, to looks so inspired with pitying sympathy, produced no effect on Wm. They did produce an effect. He grew calmer, rested his face on her shoulder, and lay still in her arms. The balmy words being repeated, he again pillowed his cheek on her breast, and was again tranquil.
如果他的母亲用这么低沉、这么甜蜜的语调向他讲述这些考虑和理由，再加上她那么亲切而温柔的抚摸和那么怜悯同情的神情，如果对维克托还毫无效果的话，那维克托就不配做他父亲真正的儿子。这确实产生了效果。他逐渐平静下来，脸埋在母亲的肩膀上，安静地趴在她的怀里。不一会儿他抬起头，让他的母亲再跟他说一遍约克是毫无痛苦地死去的，他的父亲并不残忍。母亲又重复了一遍这些温和的话语，他再次将他的脸颊贴在母亲的胸前，又平静下来。
Some hours after, he came to me in my library, asked if I forgave him, and desired to be reconciled. I drew the lad to my side, and there I kept him a good while, and had much talk with him, in the course of which he disclosed many points of feeling and thought I approved of in my son. I found, it is true, few elements of the 'good fellow' or the 'fine fellow' in him, scant sparkles of the spirit which loves to flash over the wine cup, or which kindles the passions to a destroying fire; but I saw in the soil of his heart healthy and swelling germs of compassion, affection, fidelity. I discovered in the garden of his intellect a rich growth of wholesome principles; reason, justice, moral courage, promised, if not blighted, a fertile bearing. So I bestowed on his large forehead, and on his cheek—still pale with tears—a proud and contented kiss and sent him away comforted. Yet I saw him the next day laid on the mound under which Yorke had been buried, his face covered with his hands. He was melancholy for some weeks, and more than a year elapsed before he would listen to any proposal of having another dog.
几个小时之后，他来到我的书房，问我是否能够原谅他，表示想和我和解。我把儿子拉到身边，就这样让他呆了好大一会儿，和他谈了很多，在此期间他向我披露了很多方面的情感和想法，我很赞成。我发现他的身上真得没有很多“好孩子”或者“优秀孩子”的特征，也缺少那种喝些葡萄酒就灵光闪现的才能，或者那种能够能给他人熄灭的心灵火焰和带来激情的天赋；但是我发现在他心房的土壤上健康茁壮地生长着同情、喜爱与忠诚的胚芽。我发现在他的智力花园里茁壮地生长着很多有益身心的准则，如理智、公正、品性坚强，只要保持下去，不要枯萎，势必会结出丰盛的果实。所以我在他那宽阔的前额以及苍白的、还带着泪珠的脸颊上自豪而满意地吻了一下，让他得到了安慰，然后就让他走了。但第二天我还是看见他双手遮住脸，躺在约克的坟上。他伤心了好几个星期，大概一年多时间内，他完全不想听再养只狗的建议。
Victor learns fast. He must soon go to Eton, where, I suspect, his first year or two will be utter wretchedness. To leave me, his mother, and his home, will give his heart an agonized wrench. Then, the fagging will not suit him; but emulation, thirst after knowledge, the glory of success, will stir and reward him in time. Meantime, I feel in myself a strong repugnance to fix the hour which will uproot my sole olive branch and transplant it far from me; and, when I speak to Frances on the subject, I am heard with a kind of patient pain, as though I alluded to some fearful operation, at which her nature shudders, but from which her fortitude will not permit her to recoil. The step must, however, be taken, and it shall be; for, though Frances will not make a milksop of her son, she will accustom him to a style of treatment, a forbearance, a congenial tenderness, he will meet with from none else. She sees, as I also see, a something in Victor's temper—a kind of electrical ardour and power—which emits, now and then, ominous sparks. Hunsden calls it his spirit, and says it should not be curbed. I call it the leaven of the offending Adam, and consider that it should be, if not whipped out of him, at least soundly disciplined; and that he will be cheap of any amount of either bodily or mental suffering which will ground him radically in the art of self-control. Frances gives this something in her son's marked character no name; but when it appears in the grinding of his teeth, in the glittering of his eye, in the fierce revolt of feeling against disappointment, mischance, sudden sorrow, or supposed injustice, she folds him to her breast, or takes him to walk with her alone in the wood. Then she reasons with him like any philosopher, and to reason Victor is ever accessible; then she looks at him with eyes of love, and by love Victor can be infallibly subjugated. But will reason or love be the weapons with which in future the world will meet his violence? Oh, no! For that flash in his black eye, for that cloud on his bony brow, for that compression of his statuesque lips, the lad will some day get blows instead of blandishments, kicks instead of kisses. Then for the fit of mute fury which will sicken his body and madden his soul, then for the ordeal of merited and salutary suffering out of which he will come, I trust, a wiser and a better man!
维克托学得很快。很快他就必须到伊顿公学读书，我觉得他在那里的前一两年会很悲惨。离开我，离开母亲，离开这个家会给他的心灵带来极度的痛苦。况且，他不适合替高年级学生跑腿；但是竞争意识，对知识的渴望以及成功的荣耀会逐渐刺激和奖励他的。在这段期间，我自己有着很强烈的抵触情绪来确定一个时间将我的独苗连根拔起，在离我很远的地方重新种植；当我向弗朗西斯提及这件事情时，她强忍着痛苦听着，似乎我在说一次可怕的手术，她的母性本能使她感到不寒而栗，但她的坚韧不拔不允许她畏缩。但是必须得走这一步，而且马上就要走；因为虽然弗朗西斯不想将儿子培养成没有男子汉气概的人，但她要让他习惯于一种新的待人方式，习惯于自制，习惯于志同道合的友情，而这些，除了在学校外，在任何人那里都得不到。她看到了，正如我也看到了，维克托的脾气里有着一种东西——一种像电一般的热情和力量——时而出现，迸发出令人生畏的火花。亨斯登称它为他的精神，说那不应该被强加控制。我说那是惹是生非的亚当潜移默化的影响，并且认为就算不从他身上赶走，至少也要正确地加以控制；不然任何一点肉体或者精神上的痛苦都会毁掉他，而只有这些痛苦才能使他彻底地学会自制。弗朗西斯不知道该怎么称呼儿子性格里的这种特质；但是每当它出现在他咬牙切齿时，出现在他怒火中烧的眼睛时，出现在他对于失望、灾难、突然的悲伤或者自认为的不公表现出激烈的反抗时，她会把他搂入怀中，或者独自带他去树林里走走。然后她会像哲学家一样劝服他，而维克托一向能听进去这种劝导；然后她就会满眼慈爱地看着他，维克托总会被慈爱征服的。但是将来世界也会用劝导和慈爱来面对他的狂暴吗？啊，不会的！因为他黑色眼睛里的怒火，因为他消瘦的眉头上的阴云，因为他轮廓清晰的唇角上绷紧的表情，这个孩子总有一天得到的不会是温言软语而是当头痛击，不是甜蜜一吻，而是拳打脚踢。那时无言的愤怒会使他身体虚弱、灵魂发疯，我相信受点痛苦挫折是应当并且是有益的，这样会使他成为一个更为明智和优秀的人！

He says their domestic harmony is not the finest in the world, but in business they are doing "on ne peut mieux," which circumstance, he concludes, will be a sufficient consolation to both for any little crosses in the affections. Why don't you invite the Pelets to —shire, Crimsworth? I should so like to see your first flame, Zoraéde. Mistress, don't be jealous, but he loved that lady to distraction; I know it for a fact. Brown says she weighs twelve stones now. You see what you've lost, Mr. Professor. Now, monsieur and madame, if you don't come to tea, Victor and I will begin without you.' 
“我昨天在X镇。你哥哥内德做铁路投机买卖发财了，现在比克里萨斯还要富有。他们称他已经跻身股票交易大厅的十大股票投机商之一。而且我听布朗先生说。范登赫顿夫妇和让·巴普蒂斯特谈到下个月要来看你们。他还提到了珀莱夫妇。他说他们的家庭并不算世界上最和谐的，但是在事业上他们干得是“不能再好了”，他总结说，这种情况对于双方感情上的任何小的磨难都足够称得上是一种慰藉。你为什么不邀请珀莱夫妇来某某郡呢，克里姆斯沃思？我很想见一见你的初恋情人佐蕾德。夫人别嫉妒，他爱那位夫人可是爱到精神错乱呢；我可是有事实根据的。布朗说她现在重达十二英石。教师先生，你看你丢掉了什么。现在，先生、夫人，如果你们再不来用茶的话，我和维克托就要先开始了哦。”
