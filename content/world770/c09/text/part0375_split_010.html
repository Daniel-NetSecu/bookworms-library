XI  

第十一章  

Litvinov took up his book again, but he could not read. He went out of the house, walked a little, listened to the music, glanced in at the gambling, returned again to his room, and tried again to read—still without success. The time seemed to drag by with peculiar dreariness. Pishtchalkin, the well-intentioned peaceable mediator, came in and sat with him for three hours. He talked, argued, stated questions, and discoursed intermittently, first of elevated, and then of practical topics, and succeeded in diffusing around him such an atmosphere of dullness that poor Litvinov was ready to cry. In raising dullness—agonizing, chilling, helpless, hopeless dullness—to a fine art, Pishtchalkin was absolutely unrivaled even among persons of the highest morality, who are notoriously masters in that line. The there sight of his well-cut and well-brushed head, his clear lifeless eyes, his benevolent nose, produced an involuntary despondency, and his deliberate, drowsy, lazy tone seemed to have been created only to state with conviction and lucidity such sententious truths as that twice two makes four and not five or three, that water is liquid, and benevolence laudable; that to the private individual, no less than to the state, and to the state no less than to the private individual, credit is absolutely indispensable for financial operations. And with all this he was such an excellent man! But such is the sentence the fates have passed on Russia; among us, good men are dull, Pishtchalkin retreated at last; he was replaced by Bindasov, who, without any beating about the bush, asked Litvinov with great effrontery for a loan of a hundred guldens, and the latter gave it him, in spite of the fact that Bindasov was not only unattractive, but even repulsive to him, that he knew for certain that he would never get his money back and was, besides, himself in need of it. What made him give him the money then, the reader will inquire. Who can tell! That is another Russian weakness. Let the reader lay his hand on his heart and remember how many acts in his own life have had absolutely no other reason. And Bindasov did not even thank Litvinov; he asked for a glass of red Baden wine, and without wiping his lips departed, loudly and offensively tramping with his boots. And how vexed Litvinov was with himself already, as he watched the red nape of the retreating sharper's neck! Before evening he received a letter from Tatyana in which she informed him that as her aunt was not well, she could not come to Baden for five or six days. This news had a depressing influence on Litvinov; it increased his vexation, and he went to bed early in a disagreeable frame of mind. The following day turned out no better, if not worse, than the preceding. From early morning Litvinov's room was filled with his own countrymen; Bambaev, Voroshilov, Pishtchalkin, the two officers, the two Heidelberg students, all crowded in at once, and yet did not go away right up till dinner time, though they had soon said all they had to say and were obviously bored. They simply did not know what to do with themselves, and having got into Litvinov's lodgings they "stuck" there, as they say. First they discussed the fact that Gubaryov had gone back to Heidelberg, and that they would have to go after him; then they philosophized a little, and touched on the Polish question; then they advanced to reflections on gambling and cocottes, and fell to repeating scandalous anecdotes; at last the conversation sank into a discussion of all sorts of "strong men" and monsters of obesity and gluttony. First, they trotted out all the ancient stories of Lukin, of the deacon who ate no less than thirty-three herrings for a wager, of the Uhlan colonel, Ezyedinov, renowned for his corpulence, and of the soldier who broke the shin-bone on his own forehead; then followed unadulterated lying. Pishtchalkin himself related with a yawn that he knew a peasant woman in Little Russia, who at the time of her death had proved to weigh half a ton and some pounds, and a landowner who had eaten three geese and a sturgeon for luncheon; Bambaev suddenly fell into an ecstatic condition, and declared he himself was able to eat a whole sheep, "with seasoning" of course; and Voroshilov burst out with something about a comrade, an athletic cadet, so grotesque that every one was reduced to silence, and after looking at each other, they took up their hats, and the party broke up. Litvinov, when he was left alone, tried to occupy him self, but he felt just as if his head was full of smoldering soot; he could do nothing that was of any use, and the evening, too, was wasted. The next morning he was just preparing for lunch, when some one knocked at his door. "Good Lord," thought Litvinov, "one of yesterday's dear friends again," and not with out some trepidation he pronounced: 
李特维诺夫再次拿起书，但看不进去。他走出屋子，散了一会儿步，听了听音乐，瞥了一眼赌局，又回到房间，再次试图看书——但还是看不进去。时间似乎缓缓而逝，仿佛被一种怪异的枯燥攫住了一样。比夏尔金，那位好心的调停人进了房间，陪他坐了三个小时。他不停地说话，论证，陈述问题，发表评论，起先是些高雅的话题，然后是一些实际的话题，成功地将沉闷的气氛扩散到他的周围，致使可怜的李特维诺夫苦不堪言。说到给高雅艺术制造沉闷这个本事，即令人痛苦的、恐惧的、无助的和绝望的沉闷，在那些品德最为高尚的人当中比夏尔金绝对是无与伦比的，虽然这些人是这方面出了名的大师。一看到他那疏剪整齐的头发，清澈而无神的眼睛和慈善的鼻子，就使人不由自主地感到沮丧。同时，他那从容的、昏昏欲睡的、慵懒的语调似乎天生就是用来坚定清晰地表达一些简单易懂的真理，例如，二乘以二等于四，不是三或者五；水是液体的；善行是值得称赞的；不管是对个体还是国家信用都是保持经济运行必不可少的东西。虽然这样，他仍是个优秀的人！这就是命运对俄国的惩罚。在我们之中，好人都很沉闷。最后，比夏尔金离开了，宾达索夫又来了，他开门见山、厚颜无耻地向李特维诺夫借一百盾。李特维诺夫把钱借给了他，尽管他不喜欢甚至讨厌宾达索夫，也知道自己绝对要不回钱了，此外，他自己也需要钱。读者会问，是什么使他借钱给宾达索夫。谁知道呢！这是俄国人的另一个缺点。读者请扪心自问，回想一下一生当中做过多少这种毫无道理的事情。宾达索夫连谢都不谢李特维诺夫。他要了一杯巴登红葡萄酒，喝完嘴巴没擦就恬不知耻地迈着又重又响的步子离开了。看着逐渐走远的赌棍那红红的颈背，李特维诺夫是多么生气啊！黄昏时他收到来自塔吉娅娜的信，信中说她姑妈身体不大舒服，她无法在五六天之内赶到巴登。这一消息使李特维诺夫很沮丧。这增添了他的苦恼，于是他很不愉快地早早上床睡觉去了。第二天他的心情并没有转好，甚至比前一天更差。一大清早，李特维诺夫的房间里就挤满了他的同胞：庞巴耶夫、沃罗希洛夫、比夏尔金、两位官员和两名来自海德堡的学生。他们一下子全都蜂拥而至，到了吃饭的时间也没离开，尽管他们把该说的都说完了，也明显感到无聊。他们只是不知道该做些什么，于是来到李特维诺夫家，然后就像他们所说的，“黏”在那里了。首先，他们讨论了古巴廖夫已回到海德堡的事，说他们得去拜访他；接着谈了一点哲学并谈到了波兰问题；然后进一步谈到对赌博和妓女的想法以及重复上演的各种丑闻，最后谈话演变成了一场关于各种“大力士”、大胖子以及暴饮暴食怪物的讨论。他们先是搬出了一个个老掉牙的故事：卢金的故事啦，执事跟人打赌，一次性吃了至少三十三条鲱鱼的故事啦，以肥胖著称的枪骑士伊兹耶季诺夫上校的故事啦，有个士兵折断了自己前额的一根胫骨的故事啦，紧接着的就是彻彻底底的信口开河了。比夏尔金一边打哈欠一边说，他认识小俄国的一位农妇，她死的时候被证实有半吨外加几磅重，还有一位地主一顿午餐就吃了三只鹅和一条鲟鱼。庞巴耶夫突然变得欣喜若狂，宣称自己能吃下一整头羊，当然了，“要加佐料”。沃罗希洛夫突然说起了一个伙伴，一个体格健壮的军校学生的事情，这事如此稀奇古怪，致使众人皆沉默不语，看了看彼此之后，他们拿了自己的帽子，聚会结束。当只剩下他一个人的时候，李特维诺夫试图做点儿事，但是觉得脑袋里像塞满了闷烧着的煤烟。他干不出一件有用的事，晚上也就被浪费了。第二天早晨他正在准备午餐，突然有人敲门。“天哪，”李特维诺夫心想，“又是昨天那群宝贝朋友中的哪个？”于是他不无惶恐地说：
"Herein!" The door opened slowly and in walked Potugin. Litvinov was exceedingly delighted to see him.
“请进！”门慢慢地打开了，波图金走了进来。李特维诺夫非常高兴见到他。
"This is nice!" he began, warmly shaking hands with his unexpected visitor, "this is good of you! I should certainly have looked you up myself, but you would not tell me where you live. Sit down, please, put down your hat. Sit down." Potugin made no response to Litvinov's warm welcome, and remained standing in the middle of the room, shifting from one leg to the other; he only laughed a little and shook his head. Litvinov's cordial reception obviously touched him, but there was some constraint in the expression of his face.
“太好了！”他说，并与这位意料之外的访客热情地握手，“您真是太好了！本来我肯定是应该去拜访您的，但您就是不肯告诉我您住在哪里。请坐，把帽子摘下吧。请坐。”波图金对李特维诺夫的热情欢迎没有作出任何回应，仍然站在房间中央，不停地换着腿。他只微微笑了笑，摇了摇头。李特维诺夫的热情款待显然感动了他，但他的表情还是有点儿局促不安。
"There's... some little misunderstanding," he began, not without hesitation. "Of course, it would always be... a pleasure... to me... but I have been sent... especially to you." 
“有……有点小误会。”他吞吞吐吐地说，“当然，我总是……乐意……我……到这里来……但是是有人特地让我来……来找您的。”
"That is to say, do you mean," commented Litvinov in an injured voice, "that you would not have come to me of your own accord?" "Oh, no,... indeed! But I... I should, perhaps, not have made up my mind to intrude on you to day, if I had not been asked to come to you. In fact, I have a message for you." 
“也就是说，您的意思是，”李特维诺夫说，听上去有点伤心，“您自己是不会主动来看我的？”“哦，不会……的确不会！但是我……或许，要不是有人请求我来找您，我应该不会决定今天来打搅您的。事实上，我有个口信要带给您。”
"From whom, may I ask?" 
“我能问问是谁的口信吗？”
"From a person you know, from Irina Pavlovna Ratmirov. You promised three days ago to go and see her and you have not been." 
“一个您认识的人，伊琳娜·巴甫洛夫娜·拉特米罗夫。三天前，您承诺会去看她，但是您没去。”
Litvinov stared at Potugin in amazement.
李特维诺夫惊讶地盯着波图金。
"You know Madame Ratmirov?" 
“您认识拉特米罗夫夫人？”
"As you see." 
“如您所见。”
"And you know her well?" 
“您和她很熟？”
"I am to a certain degree a friend of hers." 
“在某种程度上我是她的朋友。”
Litvinov was silent for a little.
李特维诺夫沉默了一会儿。
"Allow me to ask you," he began at last, "do you know why Irina Pavlovna wants to see me?" 
“请允许我问一下，”他终于说道，“您知道伊琳娜·巴甫洛夫娜为什么想见我吗？”
Potugin went up to the window.
波图金走到窗前。
"To a certain degree I do. She was, as far as I can judge, very pleased at meeting you,—well,—and she wants to renew your former relations."  "Renew," repeated Litvinov. "Excuse my indiscretion, but allow me to question you a little more. Do you know what was the nature of those relations?" 
“在某种程度上我知道。据我判断，遇到您她非常开心，嗯，她想与您恢复以前的关系。”“恢复。”李特维诺夫重复了波图金的话。“原谅我言语鲁莽，但请允许我再问问您。您知道这些是什么性质的关系吗？”
"Strictly speaking... no, I don't know. But I imagine," added Potugin, turning suddenly to Litvinov and looking affectionately at him, "I imagine that they were of some value. Irina Pavlovna spoke very highly of you, and I was obliged to promise her I would bring you. Will you come?" 
“严格说来……不，我并不知道。但是我想，”波图金突然转身，亲切地望着李特维诺夫补充道，“我想那是些很有价值的关系。伊琳娜·巴甫洛夫娜高度赞扬了您，我不得不向她保证会把您带到她的面前。您会去吗？”
"When?" 
“什么时候？”
"Now... at once." 
“现在……马上。”
Litvinov merely made a gesture with his hand.
李特维诺夫只是做了个手势。
"Irina Pavlovna," pursued Potugin, "supposes that the... how can I express it... the environment, shall we say, in which you found her the other day, was not likely to be particularly attractive to you; but she told me to tell you, that the devil is not so black as he is fancied." 
“伊琳娜·巴甫洛夫娜，”波图金继续说，“假设……我该怎么说呢……我们可以说，前几天您看见的她所处的环境不可能对您有特别的吸引力，但是她让我转告您，恶魔并不总是像人们所想象的那么黑暗。”
"Hm... Does that saying apply strictly to the environment?" 
“嗯……这句话完全适用于这一环境吗？”
"Yes... and in general." 
“是的……大体上是的。”
"Hm... Well, and what is your opinion, Sozont Ivanitch, of the devil?" 
“嗯……那么，索松特·伊凡内奇，您对恶魔的看法是什么？”
"I think, Grigory Mihalitch, that he is in any case not what he is fancied." 
“我觉得，格里高利·米哈伊洛维奇，不管怎样它都不是人们所想象的那样。”
"Is he better?" 
“要好一些吗？”
"Whether better or worse it's, hard to say, but certainly he is not the same as he is fancied. Well, shall we go?" 
“是好些还是坏些，都很难说，但肯定和人们想象的不一样。好了，我们可以走了吗？”
"Sit here a little first. I must own that it still seems rather strange to me." 
“先在这里坐会儿。我承认，我还是觉得相当奇怪。”
"What seems strange, may I make bold to inquire?" 
“什么相当奇怪，恕我大胆问一句？”
"In what way can you have become a friend of Irina Pavlovna?"  
“您是怎样成为伊琳娜·巴甫洛夫娜的朋友的？”
Potugin scanned himself.
波图金打量了自己。
"With my appearance, and my position in society, it certainly does seem rather incredible; but you know—Shakespeare has said already, 'There are more things in heaven and earth, Horatio, etc.' Life, too, is not to be trifled with. Here is a simile for you; a tree stands before you when there is no wind; in what way can a leaf on a lower branch touch a leaf on an upper branch? It's impossible. But when the storm rises it is all changed... and the two leaves touch." 
“以我的外表和社会地位，这似乎的确令人难以置信，但是，您也知道，莎士比亚早就说过，‘霍雷肖，世界之广，无奇不有’等等。生活也同样不能小觑。这里有个比喻：在您面前有一棵树，但此时没有风，那么，低树枝上的树叶怎样才能碰到高树枝上的树叶呢？这是不可能的。但是暴风雨一来，一切都变了……这两片树叶碰到了一起。”
"Aha! So there were storms?" 
“啊！所以说，是有暴风雨了？”
"I should think so! Can one live without them? But enough of philosophy. It's time to go." 
“我想是的！生活中怎能没有暴风雨呢？但是哲理说够了。该走了。”
Litvinov was still hesitating.
李特维诺夫仍然犹豫不决。
"O good Lord!" cried Potugin with a comic face, "what are young men coming to nowadays! A most charming lady invites them to see her, sends messengers after them on purpose, and they raise difficulties. You ought to be ashamed, my dear sir, you ought to be ashamed. Here's your hat. Take it and 'Vorwärts,' as our ardent friends, the Germans, say." 
“天哪！”波图做了一个滑稽的鬼脸，叫道“现在的年轻人架子可真大！一位迷人的女士邀请他们去看她，还特意派信使来请他们，但他们还要推三阻四。您应该感到羞愧，亲爱的先生，应该感到羞愧。这是您的帽子。拿着，‘冲啊’，就像我们热情的德国朋友们说的那样。”
Litvinov still stood irresolute for a moment, but he ended by taking his hat and going out of the room with Potugin.
李特维诺夫仍站着犹豫了一会儿，但他最后还是拿起帽子，和波图金一起走出了房间。
